    Subsection 4.2.1 Graphing Constraints: Shading the Unwanted Region

    Learner Experience 4.2.1 Graphing the Fundraiser.

    The Grade 12 class in Machakos makes \(x\) mandazi and \(y\) samosas for its fundraiser (Learner Experience 4.1.2). The constraints are
    \begin{equation*} x + y \le 100, \quad 15x + 35y \le 2520, \quad y \ge 30, \quad x \ge 0. \end{equation*}
    Draw axes with \(x\) from \(0\) to \(170\) and \(y\) from \(0\) to \(110\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Draw the line \(x + y = 100\) through \((100, 0)\) and \((0, 100)\text{.}\) Should it be solid or dashed?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Test the point \((0, 0)\) in \(x + y \le 100\text{.}\) Shade the side of the line where the inequality is false.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find where \(15x + 35y = 2520\) crosses the axes. Draw it and shade its unwanted side in the same way.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Draw \(y = 30\text{.}\) Which side do you shade?
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Shade the side of the \(y\)-axis where \(x \lt 0\text{.}\) Label the region left clear R and add a key.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Is the plan \((50, 40)\) in R? Is \((50, 55)\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 4.2.2 Graphing a Constraint.

    To graph a constraint such as \(ax + by \le c\text{:}\)
    1. Draw the boundary line \(ax + by = c\) through two of its points, usually where it crosses the axes (put \(y = 0\text{,}\) then \(x = 0\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Draw the line solid for \(\le\) or \(\ge\text{,}\) because points on it satisfy the constraint, and dashed for \(\lt\) or \(\gt\text{,}\) because they do not.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Choose a test point that is not on the line: \((0, 0)\text{,}\) or \((1, 0)\) or \((0, 1)\) if the line passes through the origin. Substitute it into the inequality.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Shade the side of the line where the inequality is false: the side that is not wanted.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Do this for every constraint. Shade what you cannot have, and what is left is what you can: the clear region satisfies every constraint at once. It is the required region of a set of inequalities, called the feasible region in linear programming, and we label it R. Every graph has a key: “Shaded, not wanted; unshaded (R), the required region.”
    🔗
    🔗
    In Grade 11 you shaded the region you wanted. With three or more inequalities the shading piles up and the wanted region is hard to see, so from now on we shade what we do not want and leave R clear, as Kenyan examination papers usually do. Always say in a key which you have done.
    🔗

    Example 4.2.4 Graphing One Constraint.

    Graph \(5x + 6y \le 30\) and shade the unwanted side.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The boundary is \(5x + 6y = 30\text{.}\) With \(y = 0\text{,}\) \(x = 6\text{;}\) with \(x = 0\text{,}\) \(y = 5\text{.}\) Draw the line through \((6, 0)\) and \((0, 5)\text{,}\) solid, since the sign is \(\le\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Test \((0, 0)\text{:}\) \(5(0) + 6(0) = 0 \le 30\) is true, so the origin is on the wanted side. Shade the side away from the origin.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.6 A Boundary Through the Origin.

    The hotel of Example 4.1.6 decides it wants more than three times as much chicken as beef: \(y \gt 3x\text{.}\) Graph this constraint.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The boundary \(y = 3x\) passes through \((0, 0)\) and \((2, 6)\text{.}\) Draw it dashed, since the sign is \(\gt\text{:}\) an order with exactly three times as much chicken is not allowed.
    🔗
    The origin is on the line, so it cannot be the test point. Test \((1, 0)\text{:}\) is \(0 \gt 3 \times 1\text{?}\) No, it is false. So \((1, 0)\) is on the unwanted side: shade the side of the line containing \((1, 0)\text{,}\) below and to the right of it.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.8 The Seedlings Region.

    Graph the constraints of Example 4.1.4: \(50x + 120y \le 6000\text{,}\) \(x \ge 20\text{,}\) \(x + y \le 80\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) where \(x\) is the number of grevillea and \(y\) the number of avocado seedlings. Label R.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(50x + 120y = 6000\) passes through \((120, 0)\) and \((0, 50)\text{;}\) \((0, 0)\) satisfies \(0 \le 6000\text{,}\) so shade away from the origin. \(x + y = 80\) passes through \((80, 0)\) and \((0, 80)\text{;}\) shade away from the origin. \(x = 20\) is a vertical line; the wanted side is to its right, so shade the left. \(y = 0\) is the \(x\)-axis; shade below it. All four lines are solid.
    🔗
    R is the clear region. Its corners are \((20, 0)\text{,}\) \((80, 0)\text{,}\) about \((51.43, 28.57)\) and about \((20, 41.67)\text{;}\) Subsection 4.2.2 shows how to find them exactly.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 4.2.10 Graphing Software.

    Free graphing software such as GeoGebra draws inequalities: type 5x+6y<=30 and it shades a side of the line. Most programs shade the side that is wanted, the opposite of this chapter, so check which side it has shaded before you copy the picture, and say which in your key.
    🔗
    🔗
    Type \(\le\) as <= and \(\ge\) as >=, for example 4x+9y<=72. Give intercepts as exact values, for example 4.5.

    Draw the region R given by \(8x + 7y \le 112\text{,}\) \(2x + 7y \le 70\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\text{.}\) Label R.
    🔗

    Boundaries through \((14, 0)\) and \((0, 16)\text{,}\) and through \((35, 0)\) and \((0, 10)\text{,}\) both solid; R is the four-sided region next to the origin
    🔗

    4.

    On your graph from Learner Experience 4.2.1, is the plan of \(60\) mandazi and \(35\) samosas in R? Is the plan of \(80\) mandazi and \(32\) samosas? Check each answer by substituting.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \((60, 35)\text{:}\) yes. \((80, 32)\text{:}\) no

    🔗

    5. Challenge: An Empty Region.

    Show that no point satisfies all of \(6x + y \le 12\text{,}\) \(x + 7y \ge 90\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\text{.}\) What does the graph look like?
    🔗
    Hint.
    How large can \(x + 7y\) be when \(6x + y \le 12\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The largest value of \(x + 7y\) is \(84\text{,}\) less than \(90\text{;}\) the whole graph is shaded.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The first constraint with the axes gives the triangle with corners \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((2, 0)\) and \((0, 12)\text{.}\) At these corners \(x + 7y = 0\text{,}\) \(2\) and \(84\text{,}\) so \(x + 7y\) is at most \(84\) on the triangle. It can never reach \(90\text{,}\) so no point satisfies every constraint: after shading, nothing is left clear. A model with an empty region has no possible plan, so a condition must be relaxed.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.2 Vertices and Whole-Number Points

    Learner Experience 4.2.16 Corners of the Fundraiser Region.

    Use your graph of the fundraiser region R from Learner Experience 4.2.1, or Figure 4.2.17.
    🔗
    1. Read the coordinates of the four corners of R from your graph.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The corner near \((50, 50)\) is where \(x + y = 100\) meets \(15x + 35y = 2520\text{.}\) Solve these two equations together.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the other three corners exactly. Which two lines meet at each?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Is each corner in R?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 4.2.18 Vertices and Whole-Number Points.

    A corner of the feasible region R is called a vertex (plural vertices). Two boundary lines meet at a vertex, so solve their equations together, by substitution or elimination, to find it exactly. Reading a vertex off a graph gives only an estimate. Give vertices exactly, as fractions, or to \(2\) decimal places.
    🔗
    A vertex on a dashed boundary is not in R, although points of R come as close to it as you like.
    🔗
    When \(x\) and \(y\) count things, only the whole-number points of R (points whose coordinates are both whole numbers) are possible plans. On a graph we mark them as dots. To list them, take each whole-number value of \(y\) in turn and find the whole-number values of \(x\) that satisfy every constraint.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.19 Vertices of the Tailor’s Region.

    Find the vertices of the tailor’s region from Learner Experience 4.1.10: \(3x + 7y \le 126\text{,}\) \(x + 6y \le 64\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) where \(x\) is the number of shirts and \(y\) the number of dresses.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The two sloping boundaries meet where \(3x + 7y = 126\) and \(x + 6y = 64\text{.}\) From the second, \(x = 64 - 6y\text{.}\) Substituting, \(3(64 - 6y) + 7y = 126\text{,}\) so \(192 - 11y = 126\text{,}\) \(y = 6\) and \(x = 28\text{.}\) Check: \(3(28) + 7(6) = 126\) and \(28 + 36 = 64\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On the \(x\)-axis the cloth line comes first: \(3x = 126\) gives \((42, 0)\text{,}\) while the machine line meets the axis further out, at \((64, 0)\text{.}\) On the \(y\)-axis the machine line comes first: \(6y = 64\) gives \(\left(0, \tfrac{32}{3}\right) \approx (0, 10.67)\text{,}\) while the cloth line meets it at \((0, 18)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The vertices are \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((42, 0)\text{,}\) \((28, 6)\) and \(\left(0, \tfrac{32}{3}\right)\text{.}\)

    Example 4.2.21 Goats and Sheep.

    A farmer in Kajiado has Ksh \(35\,000\) to buy goats at Ksh \(6000\) each and sheep at Ksh \(5000\) each. Her pen has \(26\) square metres of floor; a goat needs \(2\) square metres and a sheep \(7\text{.}\) She wants at least one of each. List every plan she could choose.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the number of goats and \(y\) the number of sheep she buys. Money: \(6000x + 5000y \le 35\,000\text{,}\) that is \(6x + 5y \le 35\text{.}\) Space: \(2x + 7y \le 26\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 1\) and \(y \ge 1\text{,}\) whole numbers.
    🔗
    Take each value of \(y\) in turn.
    • \(y = 1\text{:}\) \(6x \le 30\) and \(2x \le 19\text{,}\) so \(x \le 5\text{:}\) \(x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(y = 2\text{:}\) \(6x \le 25\) and \(2x \le 12\text{,}\) so \(x \le 4\text{:}\) \(x = 1, 2, 3, 4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(y = 3\text{:}\) \(6x \le 20\) and \(2x \le 5\text{,}\) so \(x \le 2\text{:}\) \(x = 1, 2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(y = 4\text{:}\) \(2x \le -2\text{,}\) impossible.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    There are \(11\) plans: \((1, 1)\text{,}\) \((2, 1)\text{,}\) \((3, 1)\text{,}\) \((4, 1)\text{,}\) \((5, 1)\text{,}\) \((1, 2)\text{,}\) \((2, 2)\text{,}\) \((3, 2)\text{,}\) \((4, 2)\text{,}\) \((1, 3)\) and \((2, 3)\text{.}\)

    5. Challenge: A Vertex on a Dashed Line.

    In the fundraiser, the class changes “at least \(30\) samosas” to “more than \(30\) samosas”, so \(y \ge 30\) becomes \(y \gt 30\text{.}\) Which vertices of R are no longer in R? Can the class still make a plan close to them?

    Hint.
    Which boundary becomes dashed?

    🔗
