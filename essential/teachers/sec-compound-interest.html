    Subsection 8.2.1 Compound Interest Year by Year and by Formula

    Learner Experience 8.2.1 Leaving the Interest In.

    Neema puts Ksh \(40\,000\) in a bank savings account that pays \(10\%\) per annum compound interest. At the end of each year the interest is added to the account.
    🔗
    1. Complete the table for \(4\) years.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What single number multiplies the balance each year?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Check your last balance by working out \(40\,000 \times 1.1^4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. How much would Neema have after \(4\) years at \(10\%\) per annum simple interest? How much more does compound interest give?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 8.2.3 Compound Interest.

    With compound interest, the interest is added to the account at the end of each year, and next year’s interest is paid on the bigger sum. Each year the amount is multiplied by the same multiplier, \(1 + \dfrac{r}{100}\text{,}\) so after \(n\) whole years
    \begin{equation*} A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{100}\right)^n \end{equation*}
    where \(P\) is the principal and \(r\) the rate per cent per annum. The compound interest is \(A - P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The \(R\) in \(I = \dfrac{PRT}{100}\) and the \(r\) here are the same thing, the rate per cent per annum; we keep the letters in common use.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.2.4 A Fixed Deposit for Three Years.

    Ksh \(150\,000\) is put in a fixed deposit at \(8.5\%\) per annum compound interest for \(3\) years. Find the amount and the compound interest, to the nearest shilling.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The multiplier is \(1.085\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} A = 150\,000 \times 1.085^3 = 191\,593.37 \end{equation*}
    The amount is Ksh \(191\,593\) to the nearest shilling, and the compound interest is \(191\,593 - 150\,000 = 41\,593\text{,}\) Ksh \(41\,593\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.2.5 Year by Year.

    Ksh \(25\,000\) is saved at \(4\%\) per annum compound interest. Make a table of the amount at the end of each year for \(3\) years, and check the last value by the formula.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Each year, multiply by \(1.04\text{,}\) keeping cents.
    🔗
    Check: \(25\,000 \times 1.04^3 = 25\,000 \times 1.124864 = 28\,121.60\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.2.7 Interest in the Third Year.

    For the account in Example 8.2.5, how much interest is earned in the third year alone?
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(28\,121.60 - 27\,040.00 = 1081.60\text{,}\) so Ksh \(1081.60\text{.}\) This is \(4\%\) of \(27\,040\text{,}\) the balance at the start of the third year; it is more than the \(1000\) earned in the first year.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Challenge: Two Years as One.

    Interest is \(6.25\%\) per annum compound. What single percentage increase is the same as two years of this interest?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Square the multiplier.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(12.9\%\) (not \(12.5\%\))
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(1.0625^2 = 1.12890625\text{,}\) an increase of \(12.890625\%\text{,}\) about \(12.9\%\text{.}\) It is more than \(2 \times 6.25 = 12.5\%\) because the second year’s interest is also paid on the first year’s interest.
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.2 Simple and Compound Interest Compared

    Learner Experience 8.2.9 Watching the Gap Grow.

    Ksh \(70\,000\) is saved for \(8\) years at \(9\%\) per annum. Use Figure 8.2.11 or a calculator.
    🔗
    1. Find the amount after \(8\) years with simple interest and with compound interest.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the gap between the two amounts after \(1\text{,}\) \(2\text{,}\) \(5\) and \(8\) years.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Why is there no gap after one year?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Move the rate slider to \(5\%\text{.}\) Does compound interest still pull ahead?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 8.2.10 Which Grows Faster?

    With simple interest the amount grows by the same number of shillings every year, so its graph against time is a straight line. With compound interest the amount is multiplied by the same number every year, so its graph is a curve that gets steeper. At the same rate the two are equal after one year; after that compound interest is always ahead, and the gap keeps growing.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.2.12 SACCO or Bank?

    Mumbi has Ksh \(80\,000\) to save. Her SACCO offers \(11\%\) per annum simple interest; a bank offers \(10\%\) per annum compound interest. Which is better over \(2\) years, over \(3\) years and over \(6\) years?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The SACCO adds \(8800\) a year; the bank multiplies by \(1.1\) a year.
    🔗
    Over \(2\) years the SACCO’s higher rate wins. From \(3\) years on, the bank’s compound interest is ahead, and the gap grows.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.2.14 How Many Years?

    A chama saves Ksh \(55\,000\) at \(9\%\) per annum compound interest. After how many whole years is the amount first more than Ksh \(85\,000\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Try values of \(n\) in \(55\,000 \times 1.09^n\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} n = 5\amp: \quad 55\,000 \times 1.09^5 = 84\,624.32\\ n = 6\amp: \quad 55\,000 \times 1.09^6 = 92\,240.51 \end{align*}
    The amount passes Ksh \(85\,000\) during the sixth year, so it is first more than Ksh \(85\,000\) after \(6\) whole years.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Challenge: Why the Gap Starts in Year Two.

    Explain why simple and compound interest at the same rate give the same amount after \(1\) year, and show that after \(2\) years compound interest is ahead by exactly \(P\left(\dfrac{r}{100}\right)^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Expand \(P\left(1 + \dfrac{r}{100}\right)^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Both pay \(\dfrac{Pr}{100}\) in year 1. In year 2 compound interest also pays \(r\%\) on the first year’s interest.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    After one year both amounts are \(P + \dfrac{Pr}{100}\text{.}\) After two years, compound gives \(P\left(1 + \dfrac{r}{100}\right)^2 = P + \dfrac{2Pr}{100} + P\left(\dfrac{r}{100}\right)^2\) and simple gives \(P + \dfrac{2Pr}{100}\text{.}\) The difference is \(P\left(\dfrac{r}{100}\right)^2\text{:}\) the interest, at \(r\%\text{,}\) on the first year’s interest \(\dfrac{Pr}{100}\text{.}\)
