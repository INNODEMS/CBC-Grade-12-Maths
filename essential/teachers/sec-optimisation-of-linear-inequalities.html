    Subsection 4.3.1 Finding the Optimum by Sampling

    Learner Experience 4.3.1 Which Plan Earns Most?

    The Grade 12 class in Machakos is planning its fundraiser again (Learner Experience 4.1.2). They make \(x\) mandazi and \(y\) samosas, with
    \begin{equation*} x + y \le 100, \quad 15x + 35y \le 2520, \quad y \ge 30, \quad x \ge 0\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The profit is Ksh \(15\) on a mandazi and Ksh \(25\) on a samosa, so the profit in shillings is \(P = 15x + 25y\text{.}\) The corners of the region R are \((0, 30)\text{,}\) \((70, 30)\text{,}\) \((49, 51)\) and \((0, 72)\) (Learner Experience 4.2.16).
    🔗
    1. Check that the plans \((20, 40)\text{,}\) \((50, 40)\) and \((30, 50)\) are inside R, and work out the profit for each.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Work out the profit at each corner of R.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Which plan earns most? How much is the profit?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Start at the plan \((50, 40)\text{.}\) What happens to the profit if the class makes one more samosa? Can they keep doing this? Use your answer to explain why the best plan is on the edge of R, and then at a corner.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 4.3.2 Optimal Solution by Sampling.

    The point of R that gives the largest value of the objective function (or the smallest, for a cost) is the optimal solution. The value of the objective function there is the optimum value.
    🔗
    For a linear objective function such as \(P = ax + by\text{,}\) the optimum value is found at a vertex of R: the best value of a linear objective is at a corner of the region. If the lines \(ax + by = k\) are parallel to an edge of R, every point of that edge may give the same optimum value.
    🔗
    To find the optimal solution by sampling:
    1. Find the vertices of R.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Work out the objective function at each vertex.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Choose the vertex with the largest value (or the smallest, for a cost).
      🔗
      🔗
    4. If the answer must be whole numbers and the best vertex is not a whole-number point, list the whole-number points of R near that vertex (a good start is every whole-number point whose \(x\) and \(y\) are each within \(2\) of it) and work out the objective at each. Choose the best and check that it satisfies every constraint.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Check that no whole-number point of R further from the vertex does better.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Step 4 is a practical method, not a rule that always works: the best whole-number point is usually near the best vertex, but in a long, thin region it can lie further away. That is why step 5 is needed. When R is small, as in every problem in this chapter, the nearby points are enough, and you can check by working out the objective at the other whole-number points of R. A vertex that is not a whole-number point is not an answer. Rounding a vertex can carry you outside R, so check every constraint.
    🔗
    Sampling is the method you will use for every problem in this book.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.4 The Tailor’s Best Week.

    The tailor in Kisumu makes \(x\) school shirts and \(y\) dresses a week, with
    \begin{equation*} 3x + 7y \le 126, \quad x + 6y \le 64, \quad x \ge 0, \quad y \ge 0 \end{equation*}
    and profit \(P = 300x + 800y\) shillings (Learner Experience 4.1.10). The vertices of R are \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((42, 0)\text{,}\) \((28, 6)\) and \(\left(0, \tfrac{32}{3}\right)\) (Example 4.2.19). How many of each should the tailor make to earn the most?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Work out \(P\) at each vertex.
    🔗
    The largest value is at \((28, 6)\text{,}\) which is already a whole-number point. The tailor should make \(28\) shirts and \(6\) dresses a week, for a profit of Ksh \(13\,200\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.6 Cheapest Fertiliser.

    Moraa buys fertiliser for her shamba in Kisii. A bag of fertiliser A costs Ksh \(4000\) and a bag of fertiliser B costs Ksh \(7000\text{.}\) She needs at least \(7\) bags altogether. A bag of A gives \(1\) unit of nitrogen and a bag of B gives \(6\) units; the shamba needs at least \(17\) units. She buys \(x\) bags of A and \(y\) bags of B. Find the cheapest way to buy the fertiliser.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The constraints are \(x + y \ge 7\text{,}\) \(x + 6y \ge 17\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) and the cost is \(C = 4000x + 7000y\) shillings.
    🔗
    Solve \(x + y = 7\) and \(x + 6y = 17\) together: subtracting gives \(5y = 10\text{,}\) so \(y = 2\) and \(x = 5\text{.}\) The other vertices are where the boundaries meet the axes: \(x + 6y = 17\) meets \(y = 0\) at \((17, 0)\text{,}\) and \(x + y = 7\) meets \(x = 0\) at \((0, 7)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} (17, 0)\amp: \quad C = 68\,000\\ (5, 2)\amp: \quad C = 20\,000 + 14\,000 = 34\,000\\ (0, 7)\amp: \quad C = 49\,000 \end{align*}
    The cheapest plan is \(5\) bags of A and \(2\) bags of B, costing Ksh \(34\,000\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Here R has no far edge: it carries on away from the origin, and buying more bags only costs more. The minimum is still at a vertex.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.7 Beehives and Cages.

    A youth group in Kitui makes \(x\) beehives and \(y\) chicken cages a month. A beehive uses \(7\) planks and a cage \(3\) planks; the group has \(35\) planks. A beehive needs \(3\) hours on the group’s saw and a cage \(7\) hours; the saw is free for \(31\) hours. The profit is Ksh \(900\) on a beehive and Ksh \(700\) on a cage. How many of each should the group make?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The model is \(7x + 3y \le 35\text{,}\) \(3x + 7y \le 31\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) with \(x\) and \(y\) whole numbers, and \(P = 900x + 700y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1: the vertices. Solve \(7x + 3y = 35\) and \(3x + 7y = 31\text{:}\) multiply the first by \(7\) and the second by \(3\) and subtract, \(40x = 152\text{,}\) so \(x = 3.8\) and \(y = 2.8\text{.}\) The vertices are \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((5, 0)\text{,}\) \((3.8, 2.8)\) and \(\left(0, \tfrac{31}{7}\right) \approx (0, 4.43)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: the objective at each vertex. \(P = 0\text{,}\) \(4500\text{,}\) \(5380\) and \(3100\text{.}\) The best vertex is \((3.8, 2.8)\text{,}\) but the group cannot make \(3.8\) beehives.
    🔗
    Step 3: do not just round. Rounding gives \((4, 3)\text{,}\) which needs \(7 \times 4 + 3 \times 3 = 37\) planks: more than \(35\text{,}\) so \((4, 3)\) is outside R.
    🔗
    Step 4: sample the whole-number points near the vertex. Take \(x = 2, 3, 4, 5\) and \(y = 1, 2, 3, 4\text{.}\) The points of this block that are in R, with their profits, are
    \begin{equation*} (2, 1)\colon 2500, \quad (2, 2)\colon 3200, \quad (2, 3)\colon 3900, \quad (3, 1)\colon 3400, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} (3, 2)\colon 4100, \quad (3, 3)\colon 4800, \quad (4, 1)\colon 4300, \quad (4, 2)\colon 5000\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The others break a constraint (for example \((5, 1)\) needs \(38\) planks). The vertex \((5, 0)\) gives only \(4500\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 5: check. \((4, 2)\) uses \(28 + 6 = 34\) planks and \(12 + 14 = 26\) saw hours, so it is in R. The region is small, so Figure 4.3.8 shows all \(22\) of its whole-number points: none beats \((4, 2)\text{.}\) The group should make \(4\) beehives and \(2\) cages, for a profit of Ksh \(5000\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 4.3.9 Sampling in a Spreadsheet.

    A spreadsheet can do the sampling for you. Put the values of \(x\) in column A and of \(y\) in column B, one plan per row. In column C use IF to test every constraint, for example =IF(AND(7*A2+3*B2<=35, 3*A2+7*B2<=31), "in R", "out"), and in column D work out the profit, =900*A2+700*B2. Sort the rows that are in R by profit: the top row is the best plan you sampled.
    🔗
    🔗
    Type whole numbers or exact values, for example 4.5 or 13/2.

    1.

    R is the region \(8x + 7y \le 112\text{,}\) \(2x + 7y \le 70\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\) (the region of Exercise 4.2.1.3). Find the vertices of R and the maximum value of \(P = 2x + 5y\) on R.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Maximum \(54\) at \((7, 8)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Subtracting \(2x + 7y = 70\) from \(8x + 7y = 112\) gives \(6x = 42\text{,}\) so \(x = 7\) and \(y = 8\text{.}\) The vertices are \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((14, 0)\text{,}\) \((7, 8)\) and \((0, 10)\text{,}\) where \(P = 0\text{,}\) \(28\text{,}\) \(54\) and \(50\text{.}\) The maximum is \(54\) at \((7, 8)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Find the minimum value of \(C = 5x + 2y\) subject to \(5x + 7y \ge 70\text{,}\) \(9x + 2y \ge 73\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Minimum \(45\) at \((7, 5)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Solve \(5x + 7y = 70\) and \(9x + 2y = 73\text{:}\) multiply the first by \(2\) and the second by \(7\text{,}\) then subtract: \(53x = 371\text{,}\) so \(x = 7\) and \(y = 5\text{.}\) The other vertices are \((14, 0)\) on \(5x + 7y = 70\) and \((0, 36.5)\) on \(9x + 2y = 73\text{.}\) \(C = 70\) at \((14, 0)\text{,}\) \(45\) at \((7, 5)\) and \(73\) at \((0, 36.5)\text{.}\) The minimum is \(45\) at \((7, 5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Solve the jua kali model of Example 4.1.13: \(6x + y \le 96\text{,}\) \(x + y \le 26\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) with profit \(P = 450x + 250y\) shillings for \(x\) metal boxes and \(y\) jikos. How many of each should the workshop make?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(14\) metal boxes and \(12\) jikos, Ksh \(9300\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Subtracting \(x + y = 26\) from \(6x + y = 96\) gives \(5x = 70\text{,}\) so \(x = 14\) and \(y = 12\text{.}\) The vertices are \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((16, 0)\text{,}\) \((14, 12)\) and \((0, 26)\text{,}\) with \(P = 0\text{,}\) \(7200\text{,}\) \(9300\) and \(6500\text{.}\) The best plan is \(14\) boxes and \(12\) jikos, a profit of Ksh \(9300\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Find the whole numbers \(x\) and \(y\) that make \(P = 3x + 8y\) as large as possible, subject to \(6x + 5y \le 41\text{,}\) \(2x + 9y \le 32\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \((5, 2)\text{,}\) \(P = 31\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The boundaries meet where \(6x + 5y = 41\) and \(2x + 9y = 32\text{:}\) multiply the second by \(3\) and subtract the first, \(22y = 55\text{,}\) so \(y = 2.5\) and \(x = 4.75\text{.}\) The vertices are \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \(\left(\tfrac{41}{6}, 0\right)\text{,}\) \((4.75, 2.5)\) and \(\left(0, \tfrac{32}{9}\right)\text{,}\) with \(P = 0\text{,}\) \(20.5\text{,}\) \(34.25\) and about \(28.44\text{.}\) The best vertex is not a whole-number point. The whole-number points of R with \(x = 3\) to \(6\) and \(y = 1\) to \(4\) are \((3, 1)\text{,}\) \((3, 2)\text{,}\) \((4, 1)\text{,}\) \((4, 2)\text{,}\) \((5, 1)\text{,}\) \((5, 2)\) and \((6, 1)\text{,}\) with \(P = 17, 25, 20, 28, 23, 31, 26\text{.}\) The best is \((5, 2)\text{:}\) it uses \(40 \le 41\) and \(28 \le 32\text{,}\) and \(P = 31\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Find the maximum value of \(P = 2x + 7y\) subject to \(6x + y \le 48\text{,}\) \(2x + 7y \le 56\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{.}\) Which points give it? Which of them are whole-number points?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(56\text{,}\) at every point of the edge from \((7, 6)\) to \((0, 8)\text{;}\) whole-number points \((7, 6)\) and \((0, 8)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vertices are \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((8, 0)\text{,}\) \((7, 6)\) and \((0, 8)\text{,}\) with \(P = 0\text{,}\) \(16\text{,}\) \(56\) and \(56\text{.}\) Two vertices tie. The objective \(2x + 7y\) is the left-hand side of the boundary \(2x + 7y = 56\text{,}\) so every point of the edge joining \((7, 6)\) and \((0, 8)\) gives \(P = 56\text{.}\) On that edge \(y = (56 - 2x) \div 7\) is a whole number only when \(x\) is a multiple of \(7\text{,}\) so the whole-number optima are \((7, 6)\) and \((0, 8)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Challenge: Which Profit Keeps the Corner?

    On the region of Exercise 4.3.1.5, the objective is changed to \(P = kx + y\text{,}\) where \(k \gt 0\text{.}\) For which values of \(k\) is \((7, 6)\) an optimal solution?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compare the values of \(P\) at \((8, 0)\text{,}\) \((7, 6)\) and \((0, 8)\text{:}\) \(8k\text{,}\) \(7k + 6\) and \(8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\tfrac{2}{7} \le k \le 6\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((7, 6)\) is optimal when \(7k + 6 \ge 8k\) and \(7k + 6 \ge 8\) (the value at \((0, 0)\) is \(0\)). The first gives \(k \le 6\) and the second \(k \ge \tfrac{2}{7}\text{.}\) So \(\tfrac{2}{7} \le k \le 6\text{.}\) At either end two vertices tie.
