Learner Experience 5.1.3 Tracing a Leaf.

On a walk in Kakamega Forest, Nekesa picked a leaf and traced round it on 1 cm squared paper.
🔗
  1. Count the squares that lie wholly inside the outline of the leaf.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(14\) for the leaf in the figure. Learners’ own leaves give their own counts.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
  2. Count the squares that the outline passes through.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(23\) for the leaf in the figure.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
  3. Some of the squares in part (b) are almost full and some are almost empty. What single number could each of them count as?
    🔗
    Solution.
    About \(\tfrac{1}{2}\text{:}\) on average such a square is about half covered, so the nearly full ones and the nearly empty ones balance.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
  4. Estimate the area of the leaf as (number in part (a)) \(+ \tfrac{1}{2} \times\) (number in part (b)) square centimetres.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(14 + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 23 = 14 + 11.5 = 25.5\,\text{cm}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
  5. Repeat parts (a), (b) and (d) on the grid of 0.5 cm squares below. Each small square has area \(0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\,\text{cm}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(78\) full and about \(50\) part squares: \((78 + 25) \times 0.25 = 25.75\,\text{cm}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
  6. The true area of the leaf is \(25.6\,\text{cm}^2\text{.}\) Which grid gave the closer estimate? Use the interactive in Figure 5.1.9 to try other grid sizes.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For this leaf the 1 cm grid happens to be slightly closer (\(0.1\,\text{cm}^2\) out, against \(0.15\,\text{cm}^2\)): for one shape, luck plays a part. The interactive shows the trend: 2 cm squares give \(28\,\text{cm}^2\text{,}\) then \(25.5\text{,}\) \(25.75\) and \(25.75\,\text{cm}^2\text{.}\) Once the squares are small the estimate settles close to the true area and no longer jumps about.
    🔗
