    Key Takeaway 9.3.4 Quartiles of Grouped Data.

    For grouped data, the median is at position \(n/2\text{,}\) the lower quartile \(Q_1\) at \(n/4\) and the upper quartile \(Q_3\) at \(3n/4\text{.}\) \(Q_1\) has a quarter of the data at or below it. Find the class that holds the position (the first class whose cumulative frequency reaches it). Inside a class we assume the values are spread evenly from the lower class boundary to the upper, so
    \begin{equation*} Q = L + \frac{\text{position} - CF}{F} \times c \end{equation*}
    where \(L\) is the lower class boundary of that class, \(CF\) the cumulative frequency before it, \(F\) its frequency and \(c\) the class width.
    🔗
    There is no \(+1\) here: with grouped data a position is a point on a continuous scale, not the number of a value in a list, and for large \(n\) the difference is very small.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.3.5 Goats: Quartiles by Calculation.

    Estimate the median and the quartiles of the masses of the \(50\) goats at Garissa market in Example 9.2.6.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Step 1: positions. \(n = 50\text{,}\) so \(Q_1\) is at \(50 \div 4 = 12.5\text{,}\) the median at \(25\) and \(Q_3\) at \(37.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: \(Q_1\text{.}\) The cumulative frequency first reaches \(12.5\) in the class 25–29, so \(L = 24.5\text{,}\) \(CF = 10\text{,}\) \(F = 12\text{,}\) \(c = 5\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} Q_1 = 24.5 + \frac{12.5 - 10}{12} \times 5 = 25.54\ldots \approx 25.5 \,\text{kg} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 3: median. Position \(25\) is in 30–34: \(L = 29.5\text{,}\) \(CF = 22\text{,}\) \(F = 14\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \text{median} = 29.5 + \frac{25 - 22}{14} \times 5 = 30.57\ldots \approx 30.6 \,\text{kg} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 4: \(Q_3\text{.}\) Position \(37.5\) is in 35–39: \(L = 34.5\text{,}\) \(CF = 36\text{,}\) \(F = 9\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} Q_3 = 34.5 + \frac{37.5 - 36}{9} \times 5 = 35.33\ldots \approx 35.3 \,\text{kg} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.3.7 Bank Waiting Times.

    Estimate the median and quartiles of the waiting times of the \(50\) customers at a bank in Kisumu in Example 9.2.10: frequencies \(6, 14, 18, 8, 4\) for \(0 \le t \lt 5\text{,}\) \(5 \le t \lt 10\text{,}\) \(10 \le t \lt 15\text{,}\) \(15 \le t \lt 20\) and \(20 \le t \lt 25\) minutes.
    🔗
    Solution.
    These classes are written with their boundaries, so \(L\) is the number written. The cumulative frequencies are \(6, 20, 38, 46, 50\text{,}\) and the positions are \(12.5\text{,}\) \(25\) and \(37.5\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} Q_1 \amp = 5 + \frac{12.5 - 6}{14} \times 5 \approx 7.3 \text{ minutes}\\ \text{median} \amp = 10 + \frac{25 - 20}{18} \times 5 \approx 11.4 \text{ minutes}\\ Q_3 \amp = 10 + \frac{37.5 - 20}{18} \times 5 \approx 14.9 \text{ minutes} \end{align*}
    The median and \(Q_3\) are in the same class: that is fine, because the class 10 to 15 minutes holds \(18\) customers.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.3.8 A Missing Frequency.

    The lengths, in cm (to the nearest cm), of fish landed at a beach in Homa Bay are shown below. The median length is \(37\,\text{cm}\text{.}\) Find \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    A median of \(37\,\text{cm}\) lies in the class 30–39, so \(L = 29.5\text{,}\) \(CF = 5 + 9 = 14\text{,}\) \(F = 14\) and \(c = 10\text{.}\) The unknown \(x\) comes after this class, so it changes \(n = 44 + x\) but not \(CF\) or \(F\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} 37 \amp = 29.5 + \frac{(44 + x)/2 - 14}{14} \times 10\\ 10.5 \amp = (44 + x)/2 - 14\\ x \amp = 5 \end{align*}
    Check: \(n = 49\text{,}\) and position \(24.5\) does lie in 30–39 (cumulative frequencies \(14\) and \(28\)).
    🔗

    6. Challenge: A Missing Frequency in the Median Class.

    The times, in minutes, that \(4 + 9 + x + 10 + 5\) learners took to finish a puzzle are grouped as \(0 \le t \lt 10\text{,}\) \(10 \le t \lt 20\text{,}\) \(20 \le t \lt 30\text{,}\) \(30 \le t \lt 40\) and \(40 \le t \lt 50\text{,}\) with frequencies \(4, 9, x, 10, 5\text{.}\) The median is \(26\) minutes and lies in the class \(20 \le t \lt 30\text{.}\) Find \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    \(x\) appears twice: in \(n\) and as the frequency \(F\) of the median class.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(x = 10\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(n = 28 + x\text{,}\) \(L = 20\text{,}\) \(CF = 13\text{,}\) \(F = x\text{:}\) \(26 = 20 + \dfrac{(28 + x)/2 - 13}{x} \times 10\text{.}\) So \(6x = 10\left(\dfrac{28 + x}{2} - 13\right) = 5x + 10\text{,}\) giving \(x = 10\text{.}\) Check: \(n = 38\text{,}\) position \(19\) lies between the cumulative frequencies \(13\) and \(23\text{.}\)

    Key Takeaway 9.3.14 Reading the Curve.

    To estimate a median or quartile from a cumulative frequency curve, find its position (\(n/2\text{,}\) \(n/4\) or \(3n/4\)) on the cumulative frequency axis, go across to the curve, then down to the horizontal axis.
    🔗
    The interquartile range is \(Q_3 - Q_1\text{,}\) the spread of the middle half of the data. The quartile deviation (or semi-interquartile range) is half of it, \(\dfrac{Q_3 - Q_1}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.3.16 Goats: Quartiles from the Curve.

    Use the cumulative frequency curve of the goat masses (Figure 9.2.9) to estimate the median, the quartiles, the interquartile range and the quartile deviation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(n = 50\text{,}\) go across from \(12.5\text{,}\) \(25\) and \(37.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(Q_1 \approx 25.5\,\text{kg}\text{,}\) median \(\approx 30.6\,\text{kg}\) and \(Q_3 \approx 35.3\,\text{kg}\text{,}\) the same as the values calculated in Example 9.3.5. So the interquartile range is about \(35.33 - 25.54 = 9.8\,\text{kg}\) and the quartile deviation about \(4.9\,\text{kg}\text{.}\) The middle half of the goats weigh between about \(25.5\) and \(35.3\,\text{kg}\text{.}\)

    4. Challenge: Why the Curve Agrees with the Formula.

    Explain why a reading from a cumulative frequency curve drawn with straight segments always equals the value from the formula \(L + \dfrac{\text{position} - CF}{F} \times c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Look at the segment across the class that holds the position, and use similar triangles.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Across one class the segment rises \(F\) while it moves \(c\text{;}\) going up \(\text{position} - CF\) from the start of the class moves across \(\dfrac{\text{position} - CF}{F} \times c\text{,}\) which is the formula.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The segment for the class joins \((L, CF)\) to \((L + c, CF + F)\text{.}\) A point on it at height \(\text{position}\) has risen \(\text{position} - CF\) out of \(F\text{,}\) so by similar triangles it has moved the same fraction of \(c\) across: its horizontal coordinate is \(L + \dfrac{\text{position} - CF}{F} \times c\text{.}\) A straight segment is exactly the even spread inside a class that the formula assumes.
