    Subsection 4.4.1 Solving a Problem: Maximum Profit

    Learner Experience 4.4.1 Maize or Beans?

    A farmer in Trans Nzoia has \(10\) hectares of land for maize and beans this season. A hectare of maize needs \(7\) person-days of labour and a hectare of beans \(12\) person-days; she can hire \(100\) person-days in all. She has promised a buyer the beans from at least \(2\) hectares. She expects a profit of Ksh \(40\,000\) a hectare on maize and Ksh \(50\,000\) a hectare on beans. Let \(x\) be the number of hectares of maize and \(y\) the number of hectares of beans.
    🔗
    1. Write the constraints and the profit \(P\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Graph the constraints, shading the unwanted side of each line.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Check your graph against Figure 4.4.2. Find the vertices of R exactly.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Work out \(P\) at each vertex.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. What should the farmer plant? Check that your plan keeps every limit.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Must \(x\) and \(y\) be whole numbers here?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 4.4.3 Solving a Linear Programming Problem.

    1. Model. Define \(x\) and \(y\) in words; write every constraint and the objective function; say whether \(x\) and \(y\) must be whole numbers.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Graph. Draw each boundary, shade its unwanted side, and label R.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Vertices. Solve the equations of the two boundaries that meet at each corner of R.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Test. Work out the objective at each vertex. If whole numbers are needed and the best vertex is not a whole-number point, sample the whole-number points of R near it, then check that no whole-number point further away does better.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Answer and check. State the plan in the words of the question, with its profit or cost, and check it against every constraint. Ask whether the answer makes sense in the real situation.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    These are the steps of any mathematical model: describe the situation in symbols, work with the symbols, turn the result back into words, and check it.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.4 Tables and Benches.

    A workshop in Eldoret makes tables and benches. A table uses \(5\) planks and a bench \(7\) planks; there are \(37\) planks in stock this week. A table needs \(1\) hour of finishing and a bench \(7\) hours; the finisher has \(27\) hours. The profit is Ksh \(500\) on a table and Ksh \(800\) on a bench. How many of each should the workshop make?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Model. Let \(x\) be the number of tables and \(y\) the number of benches made this week. Planks: \(5x + 7y \le 37\text{.}\) Finishing: \(x + 7y \le 27\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers. Maximise \(P = 500x + 800y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Vertices. Subtracting \(x + 7y = 27\) from \(5x + 7y = 37\) gives \(4x = 10\text{,}\) so \(x = 2.5\) and \(y = 3.5\text{.}\) The other vertices are \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((7.4, 0)\) and \(\left(0, \tfrac{27}{7}\right) \approx (0, 3.86)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Test. \(P = 0\text{,}\) \(3700\text{,}\) \(4050\) and about \(3086\text{.}\) The best vertex is \((2.5, 3.5)\text{,}\) which is not a whole-number point. Rounding up to \((3, 4)\) needs \(15 + 28 = 43\) planks, so it is outside R. Sample the whole-number points near the vertex:
    \begin{equation*} (2, 3)\colon 3400, \quad (3, 3)\colon 3900, \quad (4, 2)\colon 3600, \quad (4, 1)\colon 2800. \end{equation*}
    The point \((2, 4)\) needs \(38\) planks and \((5, 2)\) needs \(39\text{,}\) so they are outside R.
    🔗
    Answer and check. The workshop should make \(3\) tables and \(3\) benches, for a profit of Ksh \(3900\text{.}\) Check: \(15 + 21 = 36 \le 37\) planks and \(3 + 21 = 24 \le 27\) hours.
    🔗

    Example 4.4.5 School Funds Day.

    For a school funds day, the student council buys crates of soda at Ksh \(700\) each, which make a profit of Ksh \(400\) each, and cartons of juice at Ksh \(900\) each, which make a profit of Ksh \(450\) each. It has Ksh \(6300\) to spend and space in the store for \(8\) crates and cartons. What should it buy?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the number of crates of soda and \(y\) the number of cartons of juice. Money: \(700x + 900y \le 6300\text{,}\) that is \(7x + 9y \le 63\text{.}\) Space: \(x + y \le 8\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers. Maximise \(P = 400x + 450y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The sloping boundaries meet where \(7x + 9(8 - x) = 63\text{,}\) so \(2x = 9\text{:}\) \((4.5, 3.5)\text{.}\) The vertices are \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((8, 0)\text{,}\) \((4.5, 3.5)\) and \((0, 7)\text{,}\) with \(P = 0\text{,}\) \(3200\text{,}\) \(3375\) and \(3150\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The best vertex is not a whole-number point. \((4, 4)\) costs Ksh \(6400\text{,}\) more than the money available. Near the vertex: \((4, 3)\colon 2950\text{,}\) \((5, 3)\colon 3350\text{,}\) \((3, 4)\colon 3000\text{,}\) \((5, 2)\colon 2900\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The council should buy \(5\) crates of soda and \(3\) cartons of juice, for a profit of Ksh \(3350\text{.}\) Check: \(3500 + 2700 = 6200 \le 6300\) shillings and \(8\) items.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Type \(\le\) as <= and \(\ge\) as >=, for example 4x+9y<=72. Use the letters x and y as defined in the question. Type numbers of items as whole numbers.

    Subsection 4.4.2 Minimum Cost and Least Waste

    Learner Experience 4.4.8 Storing the Harvest.

    A farmers’ cooperative in Bungoma must store at least \(60\) tonnes of maize after the harvest. A small store holds \(3\) tonnes and costs Ksh \(6000\) for the season; a large store holds \(7\) tonnes and costs Ksh \(13\,000\text{.}\) The cooperative has \(14\) watchmen, one for each store, and only \(6\) large stores can be hired. Let \(x\) be the number of small stores and \(y\) the number of large stores hired.
    🔗
    1. Write the constraints and the cost \(C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Graph the constraints and find the vertices of R.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Check your graph against Figure 4.4.9. Work out \(C\) at each vertex. What should the cooperative hire?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Why does the answer use every large store?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 4.4.10 Minimum Cost and Least Waste.

    To minimise a cost \(C = ax + by\text{,}\) follow the same five steps and choose the vertex of R with the smallest value of \(C\text{.}\) In a cost problem R often has no far edge, because buying more is always possible; the smallest cost is still at a vertex.
    🔗
    When the unknowns must be whole numbers, an “at least” condition cannot be met by rounding down: rounding a vertex down can leave too little. Round up, or sample the whole-number points of R near the vertex, and check every constraint.
    🔗
    Sometimes the aim is to waste as little as possible: food left over, offcuts of timber, empty seats. The waste is the amount made beyond what is needed, and it is an objective function too. Making the most of what you have is the same as making the least waste.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.11 Feeding Learners Without Waste.

    The kitchen of a boarding school in Kitui cooks lunch for \(620\) learners. A pot of githeri serves \(70\) learners and a pot of rice serves \(45\text{.}\) A pot of githeri needs \(7\) bundles of firewood and a pot of rice \(2\) bundles; the kitchen has \(50\) bundles. Any food beyond \(620\) servings is wasted. How many pots of each should the cooks prepare?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the number of pots of githeri and \(y\) the number of pots of rice. Enough food: \(70x + 45y \ge 620\text{.}\) Firewood: \(7x + 2y \le 50\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers. The servings left over are \(W = 70x + 45y - 620\text{;}\) minimise \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Every plan on the line \(70x + 45y = 620\) wastes nothing, so look for a whole-number point on it. Dividing by \(5\text{,}\) \(14x + 9y = 124\text{.}\) Try \(x = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\text{:}\) \(124 - 14x\) must be a multiple of \(9\text{.}\) Only \(x = 5\) works (\(124 - 70 = 54\)), giving \(y = 6\text{.}\) Firewood: \(35 + 12 = 47 \le 50\text{,}\) so \((5, 6)\) is in R.
    🔗
    The cooks should prepare \(5\) pots of githeri and \(6\) pots of rice: exactly \(350 + 270 = 620\) servings, with nothing wasted. By comparison, rice alone needs \(14\) pots (\(630\) servings, \(10\) wasted), and \((2, 11)\) wastes \(15\text{.}\)

    Example 4.4.12 A Cheaper Dairy Ration.

    A dairy farmer in Nyeri mixes maize germ (Ksh \(40\) a kg) and cotton seed cake (Ksh \(60\) a kg) for her cows each day. A kg of maize germ gives \(1\) unit of protein and \(7\) units of energy; a kg of cake gives \(6\) units of protein and \(4\) units of energy. The cows need at least \(18\) units of protein and \(50\) units of energy a day. Find the cheapest mix.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the mass of maize germ and \(y\) the mass of cake, in kg a day. Protein: \(x + 6y \ge 18\text{.}\) Energy: \(7x + 4y \ge 50\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\) (masses need not be whole). Minimise \(C = 40x + 60y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The two lines meet where \(x = 18 - 6y\) and \(7(18 - 6y) + 4y = 50\text{,}\) so \(38y = 76\text{,}\) \(y = 2\text{,}\) \(x = 6\text{.}\) R has no far edge; its vertices are \((18, 0)\text{,}\) \((6, 2)\) and \((0, 12.5)\text{,}\) with \(C = 720\text{,}\) \(360\) and \(750\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The cheapest mix is \(6\,\text{kg}\) of maize germ and \(2\,\text{kg}\) of cake, costing Ksh \(360\) a day. Check: protein \(6 + 12 = 18\text{,}\) energy \(42 + 8 = 50\text{.}\)

    Linear Programming Around Us.

    Linear programming helps people across Kenya decide how to use what they have. County agriculture offices plan how to share fertiliser among wards so that the most land is covered for the money. School feeding programmes plan meals that meet learners’ needs within a budget. Matatu and lorry owners choose how many of each vehicle to run so that every passenger and every load is carried at least cost. Jua kali artisans plan how to cut steel sheets and timber so that fewer offcuts are thrown away, and farmers sharing an irrigation scheme plan how much water each crop gets.
    🔗
    Real problems often have many unknowns and hundreds of constraints. They cannot be drawn on a graph, so they are solved by computer, but the ideas are the ones in this chapter: constraints from the limits, an objective to make best, and an optimum at a corner of the feasible region. Using resources carefully, and wasting less, is good for a business, a school and the environment. Linear programming answers the question of this chapter: how can we make the best use of money, time and materials that are limited?

    Solve the tour model of Example 4.1.15: minimise \(C = 9000x + 30\,000y\) subject to \(7x + 40y \ge 160\text{,}\) \(y \le 2\text{,}\) \(x + y \le 14\text{,}\) whole numbers.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(12\) bandas and \(2\) dormitory blocks, Ksh \(168\,000\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vertices are \(\left(\tfrac{80}{7}, 2\right) \approx (11.43, 2)\text{,}\) \((12, 2)\) and \(\left(\tfrac{400}{33}, \tfrac{62}{33}\right) \approx (12.12, 1.88)\text{.}\) The cheapest is \((11.43, 2)\text{,}\) about Ksh \(162\,857\text{,}\) but it is not whole. Rounding the bandas up gives \((12, 2)\text{:}\) \(84 + 80 = 164 \ge 160\) beds, \(14\) caretakers, Ksh \(108\,000 + 60\,000 = 168\,000\text{.}\) No whole-number point of R is cheaper.

    3.

    Find the minimum of \(C = 30x + 80y\) subject to \(2x + 7y \ge 28\text{,}\) \(4x + 9y \ge 46\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(370\text{,}\) at \((7, 2)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Double the first: \(4x + 14y = 56\text{;}\) subtract \(4x + 9y = 46\text{:}\) \(5y = 10\text{,}\) \(y = 2\text{,}\) \(x = 7\text{.}\) The vertices are \((14, 0)\text{,}\) \((7, 2)\) and \(\left(0, \tfrac{46}{9}\right)\text{,}\) with \(C = 420\text{,}\) \(370\) and about \(409\text{.}\) The minimum is \(370\) at \((7, 2)\text{.}\)

    5. Challenge: More Firewood.

    The kitchen of Example 4.4.11 now has \(60\) bundles of firewood. Is there still a plan with no food left over? Is there more than one?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Which whole numbers satisfy \(70x + 45y = 620\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Yes, \((5, 6)\text{,}\) and it is the only one.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A plan with no waste must satisfy \(14x + 9y = 124\text{.}\) Trying \(x = 0\) to \(8\text{,}\) only \(x = 5\) makes \(124 - 14x\) a multiple of \(9\text{,}\) so \((5, 6)\) is the only whole-number solution. It uses \(47\) bundles, within \(60\text{.}\) More firewood allows more plans, such as \((7, 3)\text{,}\) which uses \(55\) bundles and wastes \(5\) servings, but none with no waste except \((5, 6)\text{.}\)
