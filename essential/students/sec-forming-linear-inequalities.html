    Key Inquiry Questions 4.1.1.

    How can we make the best use of money, time and materials that are limited?
    🔗
    🔗
    A Grade 12 class running a fundraiser has only so much money for ingredients and so many hours to cook. A farmer has a fixed area of land and a fixed number of workers. A transporter has a few vehicles and a load that must be moved. Each of them has limits, and each has a goal: the most profit, the least cost or the least waste. Linear programming is the mathematics of choosing the best plan inside such limits.
    🔗
    We work in four steps, one for each section of this chapter. First we form the limits as inequalities in two unknowns. Then we graph them, so that every possible plan is a point of one region. Next we optimise: we find the point of that region that does best. Finally we apply the answer to the real situation and check that it makes sense there. These are the steps of any mathematical model: describe the situation in symbols, work with the symbols, turn the result back into words, and check it.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.1 Constraints from Words

    Learner Experience 4.1.2 Planning the Fundraiser.

    A Grade 12 class in Machakos will cook mandazi and samosas to sell at a school fundraiser. The cooks have time to make at most \(100\) items altogether. Ingredients cost Ksh \(15\) for a mandazi and Ksh \(35\) for a samosa, and the class has Ksh \(2520\) to spend. Samosas sell fast, so the class wants at least \(30\) of them.
    🔗
    1. Choose letters for the two numbers the class must decide, and say what each one means.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Write an inequality for each limit: the time, the money and the number of samosas. What else must be true of \(x\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Test each plan against your inequalities. Which plans are possible? For each plan that is not, say which limit it breaks.
      🔗
      1. \(60\) mandazi and \(30\) samosas
        🔗
        🔗
      2. \(50\) mandazi and \(55\) samosas
        🔗
        🔗
      3. \(20\) mandazi and \(25\) samosas
        🔗
        🔗
      4. \(30\) mandazi and \(70\) samosas
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Could the class plan to make \(40.5\) mandazi? Explain.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Write one more limit that your own class would have, in words and as an inequality.
      🔗
      🔗

    Key Takeaway 4.1.3 Constraints.

    In a linear programming problem the two unknowns are always called \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) and each is defined in words with its unit and time period, for example “let \(x\) be the number of shirts made in a week”. Each limit on what is possible becomes one inequality, called a constraint. The words of the limit decide the sign:
    🔗
    • “at most”, “not more than”, “no more than”, “cannot exceed”, “a maximum of” give \(\le\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • “at least”, “not less than”, “a minimum of” give \(\ge\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • “more than”, “exceeds” give \(\gt\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • “less than”, “fewer than” give \(\lt\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • “\(k\) times as many A as B” gives A \(\ge k \times\) B. Check with a number: if there are \(10\) of B, there must be at least \(10k\) of A.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Numbers of things cannot be negative, so every model includes the non-negativity constraints \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\text{.}\) When \(x\) and \(y\) count things (people, vehicles, items, animals) we also say in words that they are whole numbers. Areas, masses and volumes need not be whole numbers.
    🔗
    🔗
    You formed inequalities in two unknowns in Chapter 3; here each situation has several of them at once.
    🔗

    Example 4.1.4 Seedlings for the School Compound.

    The environment club of a school is buying tree seedlings for the school compound. Grevillea seedlings cost Ksh \(50\) each and avocado seedlings Ksh \(120\) each, and the club has Ksh \(6000\text{.}\) The club wants at least \(20\) grevillea for a windbreak, and there is space for \(80\) seedlings at most. Write the constraints.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the number of grevillea seedlings and \(y\) the number of avocado seedlings bought.
    🔗
    • Money: the seedlings cost \(50x + 120y\) shillings, which is at most \(6000\text{,}\) so \(50x + 120y \le 6000\text{.}\) Dividing by \(10\) gives \(5x + 12y \le 600\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • At least \(20\) grevillea: \(x \ge 20\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Space for at most \(80\text{:}\) \(x + y \le 80\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Non-negativity: \(y \ge 0\text{.}\) (There is no need to write \(x \ge 0\) as well, since \(x \ge 20\text{.}\))
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    \(x\) and \(y\) are whole numbers, because seedlings are counted.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.5 A Tuk-Tuk Driver’s Day.

    A tuk-tuk driver in Malindi charges Ksh \(100\) for a short trip and Ksh \(350\) for a long trip. Today he wants to earn more than Ksh \(2000\text{,}\) and he has fuel for fewer than \(24\) trips. Write the constraints, and check whether \(10\) short trips and \(5\) long trips would do.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the number of short trips and \(y\) the number of long trips today.
    🔗
    Earnings: \(100x + 350y \gt 2000\text{,}\) or, dividing by \(50\text{,}\) \(2x + 7y \gt 40\text{.}\) Fuel: \(x + y \lt 24\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers.
    🔗
    For \(x = 10\text{,}\) \(y = 5\text{:}\) earnings \(100 \times 10 + 350 \times 5 = 2750 \gt 2000\text{,}\) and \(10 + 5 = 15 \lt 24\) trips. Both hold, so this plan works.
    🔗
    The words “more than” and “fewer than” give strict inequalities: earning exactly Ksh \(2000\text{,}\) or making exactly \(24\) trips, is not allowed.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.6 Chicken and Beef for a Hotel.

    A hotel in Naivasha orders beef and chicken each week. It wants at least three times as much chicken as beef, at most \(60\,\text{kg}\) of meat altogether, and at least \(10\,\text{kg}\) of beef. Write the constraints. Would \(12\,\text{kg}\) of beef and \(40\,\text{kg}\) of chicken do? Would \(15\,\text{kg}\) of beef and \(40\,\text{kg}\) of chicken?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the mass of beef and \(y\) the mass of chicken ordered in a week, both in kg.
    🔗
    Three times as much chicken as beef: \(y \ge 3x\text{.}\) Check with a number: with \(x = 12\,\text{kg}\) of beef the hotel needs at least \(36\,\text{kg}\) of chicken, and \(y \ge 3 \times 12 = 36\) says exactly that.
    🔗
    At most \(60\,\text{kg}\text{:}\) \(x + y \le 60\text{.}\) At least \(10\,\text{kg}\) of beef: \(x \ge 10\text{.}\) Also \(y \ge 0\text{.}\) Masses need not be whole numbers.
    🔗
    For \((12, 40)\text{:}\) \(40 \ge 36\text{,}\) \(12 + 40 = 52 \le 60\text{,}\) \(12 \ge 10\text{.}\) All hold, so this order works.
    🔗
    For \((15, 40)\text{:}\) \(3 \times 15 = 45\) and \(40 \lt 45\text{,}\) so there is not enough chicken. This order does not work.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Warning 4.1.7 Times as Many.

    “At least three times as much chicken as beef” is \(y \ge 3x\text{,}\) not \(3y \ge x\text{.}\) The second says the beef is at most three times the chicken, which is a different condition. Always put a number in to check: \(10\,\text{kg}\) of beef needs at least \(30\,\text{kg}\) of chicken.
    🔗
    🔗
    Type \(\le\) as <= and \(\ge\) as >=, for example 4x+9y<=72. Use the letters x and y as defined in the question.
    🔗

    A chama invests in \(x\) shares at Ksh \(200\) each and \(y\) bonds at Ksh \(700\) each. It can spend at most Ksh \(10\,000\text{.}\) Write this constraint in its simplest form.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(200x + 700y \le 10\,000\text{,}\) or \(2x + 7y \le 100\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.

    3.

    A hardware shop in Nyeri stocks \(x\) bags of cement of mass \(50\,\text{kg}\) and \(y\) bags of lime of mass \(45\,\text{kg}\text{.}\) The floor of its store can hold at most \(4500\,\text{kg}\text{.}\) Write the constraint.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(50x + 45y \le 4500\text{,}\) or \(10x + 9y \le 900\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The bags have mass \(50x + 45y\) kg, at most \(4500\text{:}\) \(50x + 45y \le 4500\text{.}\) Divide by \(5\text{:}\) \(10x + 9y \le 900\text{.}\)

    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    A farmer plants \(x\) kale seedlings and \(y\) cabbage seedlings. She wants at least four times as many kales as cabbages. Write the constraint and check it with a number.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(x \ge 4y\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(10\) cabbages she needs at least \(40\) kales, and \(x \ge 4 \times 10 = 40\) says this. So \(x \ge 4y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    A trader in Eldoret stores \(x\) bags of maize and \(y\) bags of beans. Her store holds at most \(60\) bags. She wants more bags of maize than bags of beans, and at least \(10\) bags of beans. Write the constraints. Does the plan \((35, 25)\) satisfy all of them? Does \((30, 30)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(x + y \le 60\text{,}\) \(x \gt y\text{,}\) \(y \ge 10\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) with \(x\) and \(y\) whole numbers. \((35, 25)\text{:}\) yes. \((30, 30)\text{:}\) no
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    At most \(60\) bags: \(x + y \le 60\text{.}\) More bags of maize than bags of beans: \(x \gt y\text{.}\) Check with a number: with \(20\) bags of beans she needs \(21\) or more bags of maize, and \(x \gt 20\) says this. Because bags are counted, \(x \gt y\) can also be written \(x \ge y + 1\text{.}\) At least \(10\) bags of beans: \(y \ge 10\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) and \(x\) and \(y\) are whole numbers.
    🔗
    For \((35, 25)\text{:}\) \(35 + 25 = 60 \le 60\text{,}\) \(35 \gt 25\text{,}\) \(25 \ge 10\text{.}\) All hold. For \((30, 30)\text{:}\) \(30 + 30 = 60 \le 60\) and \(30 \ge 10\text{,}\) but \(30 \gt 30\) is false, since the numbers of bags are equal. The plan is ruled out.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    A model has the constraints \(3x + 7y \le 90\text{,}\) \(x + y \ge 18\) and \(y \ge 5\text{.}\) Does the plan \((12, 7)\) satisfy all of them? Does \((14, 8)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \((12, 7)\text{:}\) yes. \((14, 8)\text{:}\) no
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    For \((12, 7)\text{:}\) \(3 \times 12 + 7 \times 7 = 85 \le 90\text{,}\) \(12 + 7 = 19 \ge 18\text{,}\) \(7 \ge 5\text{.}\) All hold. For \((14, 8)\text{:}\) \(3 \times 14 + 7 \times 8 = 98 \gt 90\text{,}\) so the first constraint fails and the plan is ruled out.
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Challenge: Between Two Totals.

    A matatu stage manager wants between \(22\) and \(36\) passengers in total, inclusive, on a trip. Let \(x\) be the number of adults and \(y\) the number of children. Write the condition as inequalities and explain why one inequality is not enough.
    🔗
    Hint.
    “Between” has two ends.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(x + y \ge 22\) and \(x + y \le 36\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The total \(x + y\) must be at least \(22\) and at most \(36\text{:}\) \(x + y \ge 22\) and \(x + y \le 36\text{.}\) Each end of “between” is a separate limit, so it gives a separate constraint. With only \(x + y \le 36\text{,}\) a trip with \(5\) passengers would be allowed; with only \(x + y \ge 22\text{,}\) a trip with \(50\) would be.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.2 Building a Model: Constraints and the Objective Function

    Learner Experience 4.1.10 The Tailor’s Week.

    A tailor in Kisumu makes school shirts and dresses. The table shows what each garment needs, what she has in a week, and the profit on each.
    🔗

      🔗
      🔗

      🔗
      🔗

    Key Takeaway 4.1.12 A Linear Programming Model.

    The unknowns \(x\) and \(y\) are the decision variables: the numbers we are free to choose. The quantity we want to make as large or as small as possible, written in terms of \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) is the objective function: \(P = ax + by\) for a profit, \(C = ax + by\) for a cost. A linear programming model is the decision variables, the constraints and the objective function, with the word “maximise” or “minimise”.
    🔗
    A resource table has one row for each resource, one column for each product and a column “Available”. Each resource row gives one constraint. The model is called linear because every expression in it has the form \(ax + by\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.13 Metal Boxes and Jikos.

    A jua kali workshop in Kamukunji makes metal boxes and jikos. A box needs \(6\) hours of welding and a jiko \(1\) hour; the welders have \(96\) hours a week. Each item needs \(1\) hour of painting, and the painter has \(26\) hours a week. The profit is Ksh \(450\) on a box and Ksh \(250\) on a jiko. Write a linear programming model for the week.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the number of metal boxes and \(y\) the number of jikos made in a week.
    🔗
    Maximise \(P = 450x + 250y\) subject to
    \begin{align*} 6x + y \amp \le 96\\ x + y \amp \le 26\\ x \ge 0,\quad y \amp \ge 0 \end{align*}
    with \(x\) and \(y\) whole numbers.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.15 A Night at Shimba Hills.

    A tour company in Mombasa must find beds for \(160\) tourists for a night at Shimba Hills. A banda sleeps \(7\) and costs Ksh \(9000\) a night; a dormitory block sleeps \(40\) and costs Ksh \(30\,000\) a night. Only \(2\) dormitory blocks are free, and the lodge has \(14\) caretakers, one for each banda or block that is booked. Write a model for the cheapest way to book the beds.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the number of bandas and \(y\) the number of dormitory blocks booked.
    🔗
    Beds for at least \(160\text{:}\) \(7x + 40y \ge 160\text{.}\) At most \(2\) dormitory blocks: \(y \le 2\text{.}\) One caretaker for each banda or block: \(x + y \le 14\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers.
    🔗
    Minimise \(C = 9000x + 30\,000y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In a cost problem the key constraint is usually “at least”: the company must give everyone a bed, and it wants to spend as little as it can while doing so.

    Example 4.1.16 From a Model to Words.

    A farmer takes \(x\) crates of mangoes and \(y\) crates of oranges to market on a pickup. Her model is
    \begin{align*} x + y \amp \le 32\\ y \amp \ge 3x\\ P \amp = 50x + 80y \end{align*}
    with \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\) whole numbers. Say in words what each line means.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(x + y \le 32\text{:}\) the pickup carries at most \(32\) crates.
    🔗
    \(y \ge 3x\text{:}\) she takes at least three times as many crates of oranges as crates of mangoes (with \(4\) crates of mangoes, at least \(12\) of oranges).
    🔗
    \(P = 50x + 80y\text{:}\) her profit in shillings, Ksh \(50\) on a crate of mangoes and Ksh \(80\) on a crate of oranges. It is the objective function, which she wants to maximise.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Type \(\le\) as <= and \(\ge\) as >=, for example 4x+9y<=72. Use the letters x and y as defined in the question.
