    Subsection 2.4.1 Mixtures in a Ratio

    Learner Experience 2.4.1 Costing Githeri.

    Maize costs Ksh \(55\) a kilogram and beans Ksh \(150\) a kilogram.
    🔗
    1. Mix \(3\,\text{kg}\) of maize with \(1\,\text{kg}\) of beans. What does the mix cost, and what does 1 kg of it cost?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Mix maize and beans in the ratio \(2 : 1\text{.}\) What does 1 kg cost?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Mix them in the ratio \(1 : 1\text{.}\) What does 1 kg cost?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Why is only the \(1 : 1\) mix costed by the mean of the prices?
      🔗
      🔗
    5. At home, make a small mix of your own (water and milk for chai, or maize and beans) and cost 1 litre or 1 kg of it at your local prices.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 2.4.2 Mixtures.

    A mixture is made of two or more components. When the components are mixed in a ratio, find each component by sharing in that ratio. The cost of 1 kg (or 1 litre) of the mixture is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{cost of 1 kg} = \frac{\text{total cost}}{\text{total mass}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    It always lies between the two prices, nearer the one used more; it is the mean of the prices only when equal masses are used. The cost of a mixture leans towards the ingredient you use more of.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.3 Chai for the School.

    The school kitchen makes \(40\,\text{litres}\) of milk and water for chai, in the ratio \(7 : 3\text{.}\) Milk costs Ksh \(60\) a litre (the water is not costed). How much milk is used, and what does 1 litre of the mixture cost?
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(10\) parts make \(40\,\text{litres}\text{,}\) so one part is \(4\,\text{litres}\text{:}\) milk \(7 \times 4 = 28\,\text{litres}\) and water \(12\,\text{litres}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The milk costs \(28 \times 60 = 1680\text{,}\) Ksh \(1680\text{,}\) so 1 litre of the mixture costs \(1680 \div 40 = 42\text{,}\) Ksh \(42\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.4 A Coffee Blend.

    A roaster blends \(6\,\text{kg}\) of grade AA coffee at Ksh \(1600\) a kilogram with \(9\,\text{kg}\) of grade AB coffee at Ksh \(1300\) a kilogram. What does 1 kg of the blend cost?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Total cost: \(6 \times 1600 + 9 \times 1300 = 9600 + 11\,700 = 21\,300\text{.}\) Total mass: \(15\,\text{kg}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(21\,300 \div 15 = 1420\text{,}\) so Ksh \(1420\) a kilogram: nearer the AB price, because more AB is used.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.5 Dairy Meal.

    A farmer mixes maize germ and sunflower cake in the ratio \(7 : 3\) to make \(300\,\text{kg}\) of dairy meal. Maize germ costs Ksh \(35\) a kilogram and sunflower cake Ksh \(60\text{.}\) Find the mass of each and the cost of 1 kg of the meal.
    🔗
    Solution.
    One part is \(300 \div 10 = 30\,\text{kg}\text{:}\) maize germ \(7 \times 30 = 210\,\text{kg}\) and sunflower cake \(3 \times 30 = 90\,\text{kg}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Total cost: \(210 \times 35 + 90 \times 60 = 7350 + 5400 = 12\,750\text{,}\) so 1 kg costs \(12\,750 \div 300 = 42.50\text{,}\) Ksh \(42.50\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Challenge: Equal Money, Not Equal Mass.

    Show that mixing equal masses of two foods gives the mean of their prices, but spending equal amounts of money on each does not.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Try Ksh \(600\) on each of two foods at Ksh \(60\) and Ksh \(150\) a kilogram.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Equal masses \(m\text{:}\) \(\dfrac{mp + mq}{2m} = \dfrac{p + q}{2}\text{.}\) Ksh \(600\) on each buys \(10\,\text{kg}\) and \(4\,\text{kg}\text{:}\) Ksh \(1200\) for \(14\,\text{kg}\text{,}\) and \(1200 \div 14 \approx 85.71\text{,}\) so about Ksh \(86\) a kilogram, not Ksh \(105\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.4.2 Mixing to a Target Price

    Learner Experience 2.4.8 Hitting the Target Price.

    A trader sells grade AA coffee at Ksh \(1600\) a kilogram and grade AB at Ksh \(1300\) a kilogram.
    🔗
    1. Find the cost of 1 kg of AA and AB mixed in the ratios \(1 : 1\text{,}\) \(2 : 1\) and \(1 : 2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Which mix costs Ksh \(1400\) a kilogram, and which Ksh \(1500\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. For Ksh \(1400\text{:}\) each kilogram of AA is Ksh \(200\) above the target and each kilogram of AB is Ksh \(100\) below it. How many kilograms of AB balance 1 kg of AA?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 2.4.9 Mixing to a Target Price.

    To mix a cheap grade and a dear grade so that the mixture costs a target price, balance the distances from the target:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (\text{target} - \text{cheap price}) \times \text{mass of cheap} = (\text{dear price} - \text{target}) \times \text{mass of dear} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    so cheap : dear \(=\) (dear price \(-\) target) : (target \(-\) cheap price). The same rule comes from an equation: the total cost of the mixture equals the target price times its total mass.
    🔗
    When a mixture is sold at a profit of \(r\%\) on the cost, selling price \(=\) cost price \(\times \left(1 + \dfrac{r}{100}\right)\text{,}\) so cost price \(=\) selling price \(\div \left(1 + \dfrac{r}{100}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.11 Tea Grades.

    A tea packer mixes grade PF1 tea at Ksh \(520\) a kilogram with grade BP1 at Ksh \(680\) a kilogram, so that the blend costs Ksh \(590\) a kilogram. In what ratio are they mixed?
    🔗
    Solution.
    PF1 is \(590 - 520 = 70\) below the target and BP1 is \(680 - 590 = 90\) above it. Crossing over:
    🔗
    PF1 : BP1 \(= 90 : 70 = 9 : 7\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Check: \((9 \times 520 + 7 \times 680) \div 16 = (4680 + 4760) \div 16 = 590\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.12 How Much to Add?

    A trader has \(40\,\text{kg}\) of beans that cost Ksh \(120\) a kilogram. How many kilograms of beans at Ksh \(160\) must be added so that the mixture costs Ksh \(150\) a kilogram?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) kg be added. The total cost equals \(150\) times the total mass:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4800 + 160x = 150(40 + x) \end{equation*}
    so \(4800 + 160x = 6000 + 150x\text{,}\) \(10x = 1200\) and \(x = 120\,\text{kg}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By the balance rule: \(40 \times 30 = x \times 10\text{,}\) which gives the same answer.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.13 Mixing for a Profit.

    A shopkeeper mixes rice that cost Ksh \(150\) and Ksh \(210\) a kilogram and sells the mixture at Ksh \(222\) a kilogram, making a profit of \(20\%\) on the cost. In what ratio is the rice mixed?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The cost price of the mixture is \(222 \div 1.2 = 185\text{,}\) Ksh \(185\) a kilogram.
    🔗
    Cheap : dear \(= (210 - 185) : (185 - 150) = 25 : 35 = 5 : 7\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 2.4.14 Proportion in Daily Life.

    Ratios and proportions let a group share a profit fairly by what each member put in; they let a contractor plan workers and days, and a company plan drivers so that no one drives for too long; and they let a trader price a mixture honestly, so that customers pay for what they get. How fast people and machines work decides how many are needed, and how long they can safely work.
    🔗
