    Key Takeaway 10.4.2 Mutually Exclusive or Independent?

    Mutually exclusive: the events cannot both happen in one trial, so \(P(A \text{ and } B) = 0\text{.}\) For “or”, add.
    🔗
    Independent: one happening does not change the probability of the other. This is usually true for separate experiments, such as two coins or a coin and a die. For “and”, multiply.
    🔗
    If \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive and each can happen, they are not independent: if \(A\) happens, \(B\) cannot.
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 10.4.3 Is It Really Independent?

    Weather, traffic and people’s choices are often linked. Rain on one day makes rain the next day more likely; a rider who carries too many passengers may also be one who skips a helmet. We assume events are independent only when it is reasonable, and we say that we have assumed it.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.4.4 Sorting Events on One Die.

    A fair die is rolled once. Decide whether each pair of events is mutually exclusive, independent or neither: (a) “shows 2” and “shows 5”; (b) “even” and “greater than 3”; (c) “even” and “less than 3”.
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The die cannot show 2 and 5 at once: mutually exclusive.
    🔗
    (b) 4 and 6 are even and greater than 3, so both can happen: not mutually exclusive. \(P(\text{even}) = \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) but of the scores greater than 3 (4, 5, 6), two out of three are even. Knowing the score is greater than 3 changes the probability, so they are not independent. Neither.
    🔗
    (c) 2 is even and less than 3: not mutually exclusive. Of the scores less than 3 (1, 2), one out of two is even, the same as \(P(\text{even}) = \frac{1}{2}\text{.}\) Check: \(P(\text{even and less than } 3) = \frac{1}{6}\) and \(\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{6}\text{.}\) Surprisingly, these events are independent. Decide such cases by calculation, not by feel.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.4.5 Two Dice and the Laws.

    Two dice are rolled. Find the probability of (a) a total of 2 or a total of 12, (b) a 6 on the first die and a 6 on the second, (c) an even number on the first die and a number greater than 4 on the second.
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The totals 2 and 12 cannot both happen: \(\frac{1}{36} + \frac{1}{36} = \frac{1}{18}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) The dice are independent: \(\frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{36}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) Independent again: \(\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.4.6 A Helmet Survey: Stating the Assumption.

    At a boda boda checkpoint, the probability that a rider wears a helmet is \(0.65\) and the probability that the rider is carrying more than one passenger is \(0.2\text{.}\) Assuming these events are independent, find the probability that a rider wears a helmet and is not overloaded. Is the assumption reasonable?
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(P(\text{not overloaded}) = 1 - 0.2 = 0.8\text{,}\) so if the events are independent the probability is \(0.65 \times 0.8 = 0.52\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The assumption is doubtful: riders who overload their bikes may also be the riders who skip helmets. The true probability could be different, and only data on both together would tell us.
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Challenge: Never Both.

    \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive, with \(P(A) = 0.3\) and \(P(B) = 0.4\text{.}\) Show that \(A\) and \(B\) are not independent.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compare \(P(A \text{ and } B)\) with \(P(A) \times P(B)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(P(A \text{ and } B) = 0\text{,}\) but \(P(A) \times P(B) = 0.12\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Mutually exclusive events cannot both happen, so \(P(A \text{ and } B) = 0\text{.}\) If they were independent it would be \(0.3 \times 0.4 = 0.12\text{.}\) Since \(0 \ne 0.12\text{,}\) they are not independent.
    🔗

    Subsection 10.4.2 Neither, At Least One and Exactly One

    Key Takeaway 10.4.10 At Least One.

    For independent events \(A\) and \(B\text{:}\)
    • neither: \(P(A' \text{ and } B') = P(A') \times P(B')\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • at least one: \(1 - P(\text{neither})\text{,}\) because “at least one” is everything except “neither”;
      🔗
      🔗
    • exactly one: \(P(A \text{ and } B') + P(A' \text{ and } B)\text{,}\) the two mutually exclusive orders.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Both, exactly one and neither cover every possibility, so their probabilities add to 1.
    🔗
    🔗

    Warning 10.4.11 Counting the Overlap Twice.

    The probability of at least one 6 with two dice is not \(\frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6}\text{.}\) Adding counts the outcome (6, 6) twice, because the two events can happen together. See Figure 10.4.12.
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 10.4.13 The General Addition Law (Extension).

    For any two events, \(P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)\text{:}\) subtract the overlap once because adding counts it twice. With two dice, \(P(\text{at least one } 6) = \frac{6}{36} + \frac{6}{36} - \frac{1}{36} = \frac{11}{36}\text{.}\) When \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive, \(P(A \text{ and } B) = 0\) and this is the addition law.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.4.14 Two Fishing Boats.

    Two fishing boats go out from Dunga beach, Kisumu. The probability that the first comes back with a full catch is \(0.7\text{,}\) and for the second it is \(0.6\text{.}\) Assume the boats’ catches are independent. Find the probability that (a) both, (b) neither, (c) at least one, (d) exactly one come back with a full catch.
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) \(0.7 \times 0.6 = 0.42\text{.}\) (b) \(0.3 \times 0.4 = 0.12\text{.}\) (c) \(1 - 0.12 = 0.88\text{.}\) (d) \(0.7 \times 0.4 + 0.3 \times 0.6 = 0.28 + 0.18 = 0.46\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Check: \(0.42 + 0.46 + 0.12 = 1\text{.}\) The boats fish the same lake on the same day, so their catches may not really be independent: a calm day may help both.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.4.15 At Least One Six.

    Two dice are rolled. Find the probability of at least one 6.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(P(\text{no } 6) = \frac{5}{6} \times \frac{5}{6} = \frac{25}{36}\text{,}\) so \(P(\text{at least one } 6) = 1 - \frac{25}{36} = \frac{11}{36}\text{.}\) Check by counting the \(11\) shaded cells of Figure 10.4.12.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.4.16 A Head or a Six.

    A coin is tossed and a die is rolled. Find the probability of a head or a 6 (or both).
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(P(\text{neither}) = P(\text{tail}) \times P(\text{not } 6) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{5}{6} = \frac{5}{12}\text{,}\) so \(P(\text{head or } 6) = 1 - \frac{5}{12} = \frac{7}{12}\text{.}\) Check with the list of \(12\) outcomes: H1 to H6 and T6 make \(7\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Challenge: How Many Seeds?

    Each maize seed germinates with probability \(0.8\text{,}\) independently. How many seeds must be planted in a hole to make the probability that at least one germinates more than \(0.99\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    With \(n\) seeds, the probability that none germinates is \(0.2^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(3\) seeds
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    With two seeds the probability is \(1 - 0.2^2 = 0.96\text{;}\) with three it is \(1 - 0.2^3 = 0.992\text{,}\) which is more than \(0.99\text{.}\)
