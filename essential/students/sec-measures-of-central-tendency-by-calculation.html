    Key Inquiry Questions 9.1.1.

    Where do we use statistics in everyday life?
    🔗
    🔗
    In Grade 10 and Grade 11 you organised data into tables and graphs and found simple averages. In this chapter you calculate measures that summarise a whole data set: where its centre is (mean, median, quartiles) and how spread out it is (range, interquartile range, variance and standard deviation). You also draw and read cumulative frequency curves.
    🔗
    These measures are used every day in Kenya. A county agricultural officer compares the mean maize yield of two wards. A school compares the spread of marks in two streams before planning revision. An M-Pesa agent looks at the typical number of transactions a day to decide how much float to keep. As you work through the chapter, look out for other places where these measures help people make decisions.
    🔗

      🔗
      🔗

    Key Takeaway 9.1.4 The Mean.

    The mean of \(n\) values is their total divided by \(n\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{\Sigma x}{n} \end{equation*}
    where \(\Sigma\) means “the sum of”. In a frequency table the value \(x\) occurs \(f\) times, so every value counts, weighted by its frequency:
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{\Sigma fx}{\Sigma f}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The deviations from the mean, \(x - \bar{x}\text{,}\) always add to \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 9.1.5 Using a Calculator or Spreadsheet.

    A scientific calculator in statistics mode finds \(\bar{x}\) once you enter the values (with their frequencies, for a table). In a spreadsheet, put the values in cells A1 to A10 and type =AVERAGE(A1:A10). For a frequency table, put \(x\) in column A and \(f\) in column B, then the mean is =SUMPRODUCT(A1:A7,B1:B7)/SUM(B1:B7). Always check that the answer lies between the smallest and largest values.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.1.6 Household Sizes.

    The number of people in each of \(50\) households in a village near Kitui is recorded below. Find the mean number of people per household.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Multiply each value by its frequency to make an \(fx\) row:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{\Sigma fx}{\Sigma f} = \frac{199}{50} = 3.98 \end{equation*}
    The mean is \(3.98\) people per household. It is not a whole number, even though every household has a whole number of people.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.1.9 Sacks of Maize.

    The masses, in kg, of \(8\) sacks of maize at a cereals depot in Eldoret are \(89, 92, 95, 90, 98, 93, 91, 96\text{.}\) Find the mean mass.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Add the masses: \(\Sigma x = 744\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{\Sigma x}{n} = \frac{744}{8} = 93 \end{equation*}
    The mean mass is \(93\,\text{kg}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.1.10 A Missing Day’s Takings.

    A boda boda rider’s takings over \(6\) days had a mean of Ksh \(1250\text{.}\) On five of the days the takings were Ksh \(1100\text{,}\) \(1350\text{,}\) \(1200\text{,}\) \(1400\) and \(1150\text{.}\) Find the takings on the sixth day.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The total over \(6\) days is \(6 \times 1250 = 7500\text{.}\) The five known days add to \(6200\text{.}\) So the sixth day’s takings were \(7500 - 6200 = 1300\text{,}\) that is Ksh \(1300\text{.}\)
    🔗

    The 100 m times, in seconds, of six runners at a sports day in Kakamega were \(12.4, 12.9, 13.1, 12.6, 13.4, 12.8\text{.}\) Find the mean time, correct to 1 decimal place.
    🔗
    Answer.

    Solution.
    \(\Sigma x = 77.2\text{,}\) so \(\bar{x} = 77.2 \div 6 = 12.866\ldots \approx 12.9\) s.

    2.

    The goals scored per match in a county league are shown below. Find the mean number of goals per match, correct to 1 decimal place.
    🔗
    Goals (\(x\)) 0 1 2 3 4 5
    Frequency (\(f\)) 6 11 9 5 2 1
    Answer.
    \(1.7\) goals
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\Sigma f = 34\) and \(\Sigma fx = 0 + 11 + 18 + 15 + 8 + 5 = 57\text{,}\) so \(\bar{x} = 57 \div 34 = 1.676\ldots \approx 1.7\) goals.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    The number of days learners in a class were absent in a term is shown below. The mean is \(2.8\) days. Find \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Days absent (\(x\)) 1 2 3 4 5
    Frequency (\(f\)) 3 \(k\) 9 6 2
    Answer.
    \(k = 10\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\Sigma f = 20 + k\) and \(\Sigma fx = 3 + 2k + 27 + 24 + 10 = 64 + 2k\text{.}\) So \(\dfrac{64 + 2k}{20 + k} = 2.8\text{,}\) giving \(64 + 2k = 56 + 2.8k\text{,}\) \(0.8k = 8\) and \(k = 10\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Challenge: Shifting the Data.

    Every height in Learner Experience 9.1.2 increases by \(2\,\text{cm}\text{.}\) Find the new mean, and explain your answer using \(\Sigma (x + 2) = \Sigma x + 2n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Write the new total in terms of the old total \(\Sigma x\text{,}\) then divide by \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(167.6\,\text{cm}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The new mean is \(\dfrac{\Sigma (x + 2)}{n} = \dfrac{\Sigma x + 2n}{n} = \dfrac{\Sigma x}{n} + 2 = 165.6 + 2 = 167.6\,\text{cm}\text{.}\) Adding the same number to every value adds that number to the mean.

      🔗

    Key Takeaway 9.1.14 Variance and Standard Deviation.

    The variance is the mean of the squared deviations from the mean:
    \begin{equation*} \sigma^2 = \frac{\Sigma (x - \bar{x})^2}{n} = \frac{\Sigma x^2}{n} - \bar{x}^2\text{.} \end{equation*}
    For a frequency table,
    \begin{equation*} \sigma^2 = \frac{\Sigma f(x - \bar{x})^2}{\Sigma f} = \frac{\Sigma fx^2}{\Sigma f} - \bar{x}^2\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The standard deviation is the square root of the variance, in the units of the data: \(\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}\text{.}\) Some books write \(s\) for it.
    🔗
    The first form shows what the variance means; the second avoids subtracting \(\bar{x}\) from every value, so it is quicker when \(\bar{x}\) is not a whole number. Keep \(\bar{x}\) unrounded in the second form.
    🔗
    🔗

    Warning 9.1.15 Divide by \(n\).

    In this book the variance divides by \(n\text{,}\) the number of values. Many calculators show two standard deviations: \(\sigma_x\) (sometimes written \(\sigma_n\)) divides by \(n\text{,}\) and \(s_x\) (or \(\sigma_{n-1}\)) divides by \(n - 1\text{.}\) Use \(\sigma_x\text{.}\) In a spreadsheet use STDEV.P and VAR.P, not STDEV.S and VAR.S.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.1.16 Passengers on a Matatu.

    The numbers of passengers on \(6\) matatu trips on Thika Road were \(14, 11, 14, 12, 9, 12\text{.}\) Find the variance and the standard deviation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Step 1: the mean. \(\bar{x} = 72 \div 6 = 12\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: the squared deviations. The deviations \(x - 12\) are \(2, -1, 2, 0, -3, 0\text{;}\) their squares are \(4, 1, 4, 0, 9, 0\text{,}\) which add to \(18\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3: divide by \(n\) and take the root.
    \begin{equation*} \sigma^2 = \frac{18}{6} = 3, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73 \end{equation*}
    The variance is \(3\) and the standard deviation is about \(1.73\) passengers.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.1.17 Shoe Sizes.

    The shoe sizes of \(40\) Grade 12 learners are shown below. Find the mean and the standard deviation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} \bar{x} \amp = \frac{301}{40} = 7.525 \approx 7.5\\ \sigma^2 \amp = \frac{2337}{40} - 7.525^2 = 58.425 - 56.625625 = 1.799375\\ \sigma \amp = \sqrt{1.799375} \approx 1.34 \end{align*}
    The mean size is about \(7.5\) and the standard deviation about \(1.34\) sizes. Keep \(\bar{x} = 7.525\) unrounded when you square it.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.1.20 Temperatures in Garissa.

    The maximum temperatures, in \(^{\circ}\text{C}\text{,}\) on \(10\) days in Garissa were \(36, 38, 35, 37, 39, 36, 34, 37, 38, 36\text{.}\) Find the mean and the standard deviation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\Sigma x = 366\) and \(\Sigma x^2 = 13\,416\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \bar{x} \amp = \frac{366}{10} = 36.6\\ \sigma^2 \amp = \frac{13\,416}{10} - 36.6^2 = 1341.6 - 1339.56 = 2.04\\ \sigma \amp = \sqrt{2.04} \approx 1.43 \end{align*}
    The mean is \(36.6\,^{\circ}\text{C}\) and the standard deviation about \(1.43\,^{\circ}\text{C}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    1.

    Find the mean, the variance and the standard deviation of \(5, 7, 8, 10, 10\text{.}\) Give the standard deviation correct to 2 decimal places.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Mean \(8\text{;}\) variance \(3.6\text{;}\) standard deviation \(1.90\)

    Solution.
    \(\bar{x} = 40 \div 5 = 8\text{.}\) Deviations \(-3, -1, 0, 2, 2\text{;}\) squares add to \(18\text{;}\) \(\sigma^2 = 18 \div 5 = 3.6\text{;}\) \(\sigma = \sqrt{3.6} \approx 1.90\text{.}\)

    2.

    The number of library books borrowed in a week by each of \(30\) learners is shown below. Find the mean, the variance and the standard deviation.
    🔗
    Books (\(x\)) 1 2 3 4 5
    Frequency (\(f\)) 4 7 9 6 4
    Answer.
    Mean \(3.0\text{;}\) variance \(1.50\text{;}\) standard deviation \(1.22\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\Sigma fx = 89\) and \(\Sigma fx^2 = 309\text{.}\) \(\bar{x} = 89 \div 30 = 2.966\ldots \approx 3.0\text{.}\) \(\sigma^2 = 309 \div 30 - (89 \div 30)^2 = 10.3 - 8.801\ldots = 1.498\ldots \approx 1.50\text{;}\) \(\sigma \approx 1.22\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Over \(6\) days, Kamau made \(18, 22, 20, 19, 21, 20\) boda boda trips a day and Chebet made \(12, 28, 25, 15, 20, 20\text{.}\) Find the mean and standard deviation for each rider, and compare them.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Both means \(20\text{;}\) standard deviations \(1.29\) (Kamau) and \(5.45\) (Chebet); Kamau is more consistent.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both totals are \(120\text{,}\) so both means are \(20\text{.}\) Kamau’s deviations \(-2, 2, 0, -1, 1, 0\) have squares adding to \(10\text{:}\) \(\sigma = \sqrt{10 \div 6} \approx 1.29\text{.}\) Chebet’s deviations \(-8, 8, 5, -5, 0, 0\) have squares adding to \(178\text{:}\) \(\sigma = \sqrt{178 \div 6} \approx 5.45\text{.}\) Same typical number of trips, but Kamau’s daily number varies much less.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    For a set of data, \(n = 5\text{,}\) \(\Sigma x = 60\) and \(\Sigma x^2 = 760\text{.}\) Find the mean and the standard deviation.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Mean \(12\text{;}\) standard deviation \(2.83\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\bar{x} = 60 \div 5 = 12\text{;}\) \(\sigma^2 = 760 \div 5 - 12^2 = 152 - 144 = 8\text{;}\) \(\sigma = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Challenge: Five in a Row.

    Show that the five consecutive whole numbers \(a, a + 1, a + 2, a + 3, a + 4\) have standard deviation \(\sqrt{2}\text{,}\) whatever the value of \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Find the mean first, then the deviations from it.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The variance is always \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The mean is \(a + 2\text{,}\) so the deviations are \(-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\) whatever \(a\) is. Their squares add to \(10\text{,}\) so \(\sigma^2 = 10 \div 5 = 2\) and \(\sigma = \sqrt{2}\text{.}\)

    Subsection 9.1.3 Median and Quartiles

    Learner Experience 9.1.24 Lining Up by Height.

    The group in Learner Experience 9.1.2 is joined by an 11th learner, of height \(161\,\text{cm}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Write the \(11\) heights in order. Which height is in the middle? Which is a quarter of the way along, and which three quarters?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Write the positions 6, 3 and 9 in terms of \(n = 11\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. A 12th learner, of height \(167\,\text{cm}\text{,}\) joins the line. Use the same formulas with \(n = 12\text{.}\) The positions are no longer whole numbers: what values would you give?
      🔗

    Key Takeaway 9.1.25 Median and Quartiles.

    Put the \(n\) values in order. The median \(Q_2\) is at position \(\dfrac{n+1}{2}\text{,}\) the lower quartile \(Q_1\) at \(\dfrac{n+1}{4}\) and the upper quartile \(Q_3\) at \(\dfrac{3(n+1)}{4}\text{.}\) \(Q_1\) has a quarter of the data at or below it.
    🔗
    A position that is not a whole number lies between two values: go that fraction of the way from the lower to the higher. Position \(3.75\) is the 3rd value plus \(0.75\) of the gap to the 4th.
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 9.1.27 Grouped Data Later.

    For grouped data (Section 9.3) we use positions \(\dfrac{n}{4}\text{,}\) \(\dfrac{n}{2}\) and \(\dfrac{3n}{4}\) instead. There the position is a point on a continuous scale rather than a count of individual values, and for the large \(n\) of grouped data the difference is very small.
    🔗

    Example 9.1.28 Walking to School.

    The times, in minutes, that \(11\) learners in Kakamega take to walk to school are \(18, 25, 12, 30, 22, 15, 27, 20, 35, 16, 24\text{.}\) Find the median and the quartiles.
    🔗
    Solution.
    In order: \(12, 15, 16, 18, 20, 22, 24, 25, 27, 30, 35\text{.}\) With \(n = 11\text{,}\) the positions are \(\dfrac{12}{4} = 3\text{,}\) \(\dfrac{12}{2} = 6\) and \(\dfrac{3 \times 12}{4} = 9\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(Q_1 = 16\) minutes, median \(= 22\) minutes, \(Q_3 = 27\) minutes.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.1.29 Pumpkins at Wakulima Market.

    The masses, in kg, of \(14\) pumpkins at Wakulima market are \(4.2, 3.8, 5.1, 4.6, 3.5, 4.9, 5.4, 4.0, 4.4, 3.9, 4.7, 5.0, 4.3, 4.8\text{.}\) Find the median and the quartiles.
    🔗
    Solution.
    In order: \(3.5, 3.8, 3.9, 4.0, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.6, 4.7, 4.8, 4.9, 5.0, 5.1, 5.4\text{.}\) With \(n = 14\text{,}\) \(n + 1 = 15\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Lower quartile: position \(15 \div 4 = 3.75\text{,}\) between the 3rd value \(3.9\) and the 4th \(4.0\text{:}\) \(Q_1 = 3.9 + 0.75 \times 0.1 = 3.975\,\text{kg}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Median: position \(7.5\text{,}\) halfway from \(4.4\) to \(4.6\text{:}\) median \(= 4.5\,\text{kg}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Upper quartile: position \(3 \times 15 \div 4 = 11.25\text{,}\) between \(4.9\) and \(5.0\text{:}\) \(Q_3 = 4.9 + 0.25 \times 0.1 = 4.925\,\text{kg}\text{.}\)

    1.

    Find the median and the quartiles of \(13, 9, 17, 11, 15, 10, 14\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(Q_1 = 10\text{,}\) median \(13\text{,}\) \(Q_3 = 15\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    In order: \(9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15, 17\text{.}\) Positions \(2, 4, 6\) give \(10, 13, 15\text{.}\)

    2.

    The masses, in kg, of \(10\) bags of beans are \(42, 35, 51, 38, 47, 40, 55, 44, 36, 49\text{.}\) Find the median and the quartiles.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(Q_1 = 37.5\,\text{kg}\text{,}\) median \(43\,\text{kg}\text{,}\) \(Q_3 = 49.5\,\text{kg}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    In order: \(35, 36, 38, 40, 42, 44, 47, 49, 51, 55\text{.}\) Positions \(2.75\text{,}\) \(5.5\text{,}\) \(8.25\text{:}\) \(Q_1 = 36 + 0.75 \times 2 = 37.5\text{;}\) median \(= 43\text{;}\) \(Q_3 = 49 + 0.25 \times 2 = 49.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    The numbers of customers per day at a kiosk in Kisumu over \(15\) days were \(34, 41, 29, 52, 38, 45, 47, 33, 40, 58, 36, 43, 49, 31, 44\text{.}\) Find the median and the quartiles, and describe the middle half of the days.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(Q_1 = 34\text{,}\) median \(41\text{,}\) \(Q_3 = 47\text{;}\) on the middle half of the days the kiosk had between \(34\) and \(47\) customers.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    In order: \(29, 31, 33, 34, 36, 38, 40, 41, 43, 44, 45, 47, 49, 52, 58\text{.}\) With \(n + 1 = 16\text{,}\) positions \(4\text{,}\) \(8\text{,}\) \(12\) give \(34\text{,}\) \(41\text{,}\) \(47\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    The eight numbers \(21, 25, 28, k, 33, 36, 40, 42\) (not necessarily in order) have median \(34\text{.}\) Find \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(k = 35\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(n = 8\) the median is halfway between the 4th and 5th values. Without \(k\text{,}\) the values in order are \(21, 25, 28, 33, 36, 40, 42\text{.}\) A median of \(34\) lies between \(33\) and \(36\text{,}\) so \(k\) must be the 5th value with \((33 + k) \div 2 = 34\text{,}\) giving \(k = 35\text{,}\) which does lie between \(33\) and \(36\text{.}\)
