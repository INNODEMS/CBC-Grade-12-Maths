    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Inquiry Questions 5.1.1.

    Where do we use transformations, and how can a matrix describe one?
    🔗
    🔗
    In Grade 10 you reflected shapes in the lines \(x = 0\text{,}\) \(y = 0\text{,}\) \(y = x\) and \(y = -x\text{,}\) rotated them about the origin, enlarged them from a centre with a scale factor, and translated them by a column vector such as \(\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) You labelled each image with primes, \(A'\text{,}\) \(B'\text{,}\) \(C'\text{.}\) In Grade 11 you multiplied a matrix by a column, found determinants and inverses, and watched a picture of a cat turn and stretch when it was multiplied by a matrix.
    🔗
    When you turn your phone on its side, the photo on the screen turns with it. A kitenge print repeats one motif again and again, turned and flipped. Both are transformations, and a computer carries them out by multiplying the coordinates of every point by a matrix. In this chapter you use matrices to describe and combine transformations, to undo them, and to say how they change areas.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.1 Images Under a Matrix

    Learner Experience 5.1.2 One Triangle, Four Matrices.

    Triangle \(ABC\) has vertices \(A(1, 1)\text{,}\) \(B(3, 1)\) and \(C(1, 2)\text{.}\) Here are four matrices:
    \begin{equation*} M_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad M_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad M_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad M_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    1. Write the coordinates of \(A\text{,}\) \(B\) and \(C\) as the columns of a \(2 \times 3\) matrix. Multiply it by each of \(M_1\text{,}\) \(M_2\text{,}\) \(M_3\) and \(M_4\) (the \(2 \times 2\) matrix on the left). Each column of the answer is the image of one vertex.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Draw triangle \(ABC\) and its four images on one grid. Label each image with its matrix.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Use the words you learnt in Grade 10 to name each transformation.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Where does the origin \(O(0, 0)\) go under each matrix?
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Work out \(M_2 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(M_2 \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) Compare your answers with the columns of \(M_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 5.1.3 Transforming With a Matrix.

    The matrix \(M = \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix}\) maps the point \(P(x, y)\) onto its image \(P'(ax + by,\ cx + dy)\text{,}\) because
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ax + by \\ cx + dy \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The shape we start with is the object. To transform a shape, write its vertices, in order, as the columns of an object matrix. Then
    \begin{equation*} \text{image matrix} = M \times \text{object matrix} \end{equation*}
    and each column of the image matrix is the image of one vertex.
    🔗
    Every \(2 \times 2\) matrix maps \(O\) onto \(O\text{.}\) So a translation, which moves \(O\text{,}\) cannot be done by a \(2 \times 2\) matrix. Every transformation in this chapter keeps \(O\) fixed.
    🔗
    The columns of a matrix are where \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\) go.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.1.5 Image of a Quadrilateral.

    The quadrilateral \(PQRS\) has vertices \(P(0, 1)\text{,}\) \(Q(2, 1)\text{,}\) \(R(3, 2)\) and \(S(1, 2)\text{.}\) Find its image under \(N = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and draw the object and the image.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Write the vertices as the columns of the object matrix and multiply:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 2 \amp 3 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 3 \amp 4 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 3 \amp 5 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    For example, the first column is \((2 \times 0 + (-1) \times 1,\ 1 \times 0 + 1 \times 1) = (-1, 1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So \(P'(-1, 1)\text{,}\) \(Q'(3, 3)\text{,}\) \(R'(4, 5)\) and \(S'(0, 3)\text{.}\) Plot both quadrilaterals and join the vertices in order. \(PQRS\) is a parallelogram, and so is its image: a matrix maps a parallelogram onto a parallelogram (unless it flattens every shape onto a line, which you will meet in a later section).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.1.6 Recognising Standard Matrices.

    Triangle \(DEF\) has vertices \(D(2, 1)\text{,}\) \(E(4, 1)\) and \(F(4, 3)\text{.}\) Find its images under \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and under \(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and name each transformation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 4 \amp 4 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ -2 \amp -4 \amp -4 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The images are \((1, -2)\text{,}\) \((1, -4)\text{,}\) \((3, -4)\text{.}\) Every point \((x, y)\) goes to \((y, -x)\text{:}\) by the Grade 10 rule this is a rotation of \(90^\circ\) clockwise about \(O\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 4 \amp 4 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \amp -4 \amp -4 \\ -1 \amp -1 \amp -3 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The images are \((-2, -1)\text{,}\) \((-4, -1)\text{,}\) \((-4, -3)\text{.}\) Every point \((x, y)\) goes to \((-x, -y)\text{:}\) a rotation of \(180^\circ\) about \(O\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.1.7 The Unit Square and the Columns.

    The unit square \(OABC\) has vertices \(O(0, 0)\text{,}\) \(A(1, 0)\text{,}\) \(B(1, 1)\) and \(C(0, 1)\text{.}\) Find its image under \(U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 2 \amp 3 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 5 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    So \(O\) stays at \(O\text{,}\) \(A'(2, 1)\text{,}\) \(B'(3, 5)\) and \(C'(1, 4)\) (Figure 5.1.8).
    🔗
    Look at the matrix: \(A'(2, 1)\) is its first column and \(C'(1, 4)\) is its second column. Since \(B = A + C\text{,}\) the image \(B'\) is \(A' + C' = (2 + 1,\ 1 + 4) = (3, 5)\text{.}\) The image of the unit square is a parallelogram.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Type one number in each box of a matrix. Type fractions with a slash, for example -3/2, or as decimals, for example -1.5.

    Solution.

    3.

    Use the unit square to name the transformation given by each matrix.

      🔗

    2. \(\displaystyle \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\)

      🔗

    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) reflection in the line \(y = -x\) (b) reflection in the \(x\)-axis (c) enlargement, centre \(O\text{,}\) scale factor \(0.5\) (d) rotation of \(90^\circ\) clockwise about \(O\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The columns give the images of \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\text{.}\) (a) \((0, -1)\) and \((-1, 0)\text{:}\) the coordinates swap and change sign, a reflection in \(y = -x\text{.}\) (b) \((1, 0)\) and \((0, -1)\text{:}\) only \(y\) changes sign, a reflection in the \(x\)-axis. (c) \((0.5, 0)\) and \((0, 0.5)\text{:}\) every distance from \(O\) is halved. (d) \((0, -1)\) and \((1, 0)\text{:}\) a quarter turn clockwise.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Explain why no \(2 \times 2\) matrix maps every point \((x, y)\) onto \((x + 2, y + 1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Every \(2 \times 2\) matrix maps \((0, 0)\) onto \((0, 0)\text{,}\) but this translation maps \((0, 0)\) onto \((2, 1)\text{.}\)

    5.

    A kitenge print repeats a motif, the triangle with vertices \((2, 1)\text{,}\) \((4, 1)\) and \((3, 3)\text{,}\) together with its mirror image in the \(y\)-axis. Write down the matrix of the reflection in the \(y\)-axis and use it to find the vertices of the mirror image.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{;}\) \((-2, 1)\text{,}\) \((-4, 1)\text{,}\) \((-3, 3)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 4 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \amp -4 \amp -3 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)

    6. Challenge: Midpoints.

    The points \(A(-1, 3)\) and \(B(5, 1)\) are mapped by \(W = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\) onto \(A'\) and \(B'\text{.}\) Show that the image of the midpoint of \(AB\) is the midpoint of \(A'B'\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 5.1.12 Finding a Matrix.

    The first column of a matrix is the image of \((1, 0)\text{,}\) and the second column is the image of \((0, 1)\text{.}\) So to find the matrix of a transformation, find where it sends \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\text{,}\) and write those images as the columns.
    🔗
    To describe fully a transformation, give every fact that fixes it:
    • a reflection: the mirror line;
      🔗
      🔗
    • a rotation: the centre, the angle and the direction (clockwise or anticlockwise);
      🔗
      🔗
    • an enlargement: the centre and the scale factor.
      🔗
      🔗
    Stretches and shears have their own lists in Section 5.5 and Section 5.6.
    🔗
    If you know the images of two points \(P\) and \(Q\) that are not in line with \(O\text{,}\) you can find \(M = \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix}\) in either of two ways:
    • write \(M P = P'\) and \(M Q = Q'\) as equations and solve two pairs of simultaneous equations, one pair for \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) one for \(c\) and \(d\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • since \(M \times \text{object matrix} = \text{image matrix}\text{,}\) multiply on the right by the inverse: \(M = (\text{image matrix})(\text{object matrix})^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In Grade 11 you met the rotation through any angle \(\theta\) anticlockwise about \(O\text{,}\) with matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta \amp -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta \amp \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Put \(\theta = 90^\circ\) and you get the matrix in the table.
    🔗

    Example 5.1.14 Reflection in the Line \(y = -x\).

    Find the matrix of the reflection in the line \(y = -x\text{,}\) and use it to find the image of \((3, 1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Reflecting in \(y = -x\) sends \((1, 0)\) to \((0, -1)\) and \((0, 1)\) to \((-1, 0)\text{.}\) These are the columns:
    \begin{equation*} M = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp -1 \\ -1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad M \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The image is \((-1, -3)\text{.}\) This agrees with the Grade 10 rule for this mirror line: the coordinates swap and change sign.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.1.15 Rotation Through \(90^\circ\) Clockwise.

    Find the matrix of the rotation of \(90^\circ\) clockwise about \(O\text{,}\) and the image of \((2, 5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    A quarter turn clockwise sends \((1, 0)\) to \((0, -1)\) and \((0, 1)\) to \((1, 0)\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} M = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad M \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The image is \((5, -2)\text{.}\) Notice that this matrix is the anticlockwise matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) with its rows written as columns.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.1.16 Matrix From an Object and Its Image.

    A matrix \(M\) maps \(P(1, 2)\) onto \(P'(4, 7)\) and \(Q(3, 1)\) onto \(Q'(7, 1)\text{.}\) Find \(M\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Method 1: equations. Let \(M = \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then \(M\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(M\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) The top rows give
    \begin{equation*} a + 2b = 4, \qquad 3a + b = 7 \end{equation*}
    so \(a = 2\) and \(b = 1\text{.}\) The bottom rows give
    \begin{equation*} c + 2d = 7, \qquad 3c + d = 1 \end{equation*}
    so \(c = -1\) and \(d = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Method 2: the inverse. The object matrix is \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) with determinant \(1 - 6 = -5\text{,}\) so its inverse is \(-\frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -3 \\ -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} M = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 7 \\ 7 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \times \left(-\frac{1}{5}\right)\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -3 \\ -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} -10 \amp -5 \\ 5 \amp -20 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Check with \(P\text{:}\) \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) So \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
