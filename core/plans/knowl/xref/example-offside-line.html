Example 7.7.10 The Offside Line.

In a simplified form of the offside rule, an attacker in the opponents’ half is in an offside position if they are nearer to the opponents’ goal line than both the ball and the second-last defender. Being level is not offside.
🔗
In a school match the second-last defender is \(12\,\text{m}\) from the goal line and the ball is \(20\,\text{m}\) from it. Three attackers are \(10\,\text{m}\text{,}\) \(12\,\text{m}\) and \(13\,\text{m}\) from the goal line. Describe the offside region and decide which attackers are in an offside position.
🔗

Solution.

The defender is nearer to the goal line than the ball, so the defender decides. The locus of points \(12\,\text{m}\) from the goal line, inside the pitch, is the line parallel to the goal line through the defender. The offside region is the strip nearer to the goal line than this line, in the opponents’ half. Its boundary is dashed, because an attacker exactly level is not offside.
🔗
The attacker \(10\,\text{m}\) out is in the strip: in an offside position. The attacker \(12\,\text{m}\) out is level with the defender, on the dashed line: not offside. The attacker \(13\,\text{m}\) out is outside the strip: not offside.
🔗
Being in an offside position is not an offence in itself; the referee also judges whether the attacker takes part in the play.
