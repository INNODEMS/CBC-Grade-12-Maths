    Subsection 4.1.2 Building a Model: Constraints and the Objective Function

    Upon completion of this lesson, learners will be able to:
    1. Know: a resource table gives one constraint for each row; the objective function is the quantity to make largest or smallest; a model is the variables, the constraints and the objective.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Do: read a table into inequalities; write the objective function \(P\) or \(C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Apply: build models for a tailor, a jua kali workshop and a tour company.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Teacher Resource 4.1.20 Problem-Solving and Discovery.

    Suggested Time

    Learner Experience 4.1.21 The Tailor’s Week.

    A tailor in Kisumu makes school shirts and dresses. The table shows what each garment needs, what she has in a week, and the profit on each.
    🔗
    Let \(x\) be the number of shirts and \(y\) the number of dresses she makes in a week.
    🔗
    1. Write the cloth constraint. Remove the decimals.
      🔗
      Solution.
      \(1.5x + 3.5y \le 63\text{;}\) doubling, \(3x + 7y \le 126\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Write the machine-time constraint.
      🔗
      Solution.
      \(x + 6y \le 64\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Write an expression for her weekly profit \(P\) in shillings.
      🔗
      Solution.
      \(P = 300x + 800y\text{.}\) There is no limit on profit, so this is not an inequality.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Can she make \(30\) shirts and \(5\) dresses? What profit would that give? Can she make \(20\) shirts and \(8\) dresses?
      🔗
      Solution.
      \((30, 5)\text{:}\) cloth \(90 + 35 = 125 \le 126\text{,}\) machine \(30 + 30 = 60 \le 64\text{,}\) so yes, with \(P = 9000 + 4000 = 13\,000\text{.}\) \((20, 8)\text{:}\) machine \(20 + 48 = 68 \gt 64\text{,}\) so no.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Which earns more, \(30\) shirts and \(5\) dresses, or \(28\) shirts and \(6\) dresses?
      🔗
      Solution.
      \((28, 6)\) is possible (cloth \(126\text{,}\) machine \(64\)) and gives \(P = 8400 + 4800 = 13\,200\text{,}\) more than Ksh \(13\,000\text{.}\) Section 4.3 shows how to find the best plan.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Teacher Resource 4.1.23 Structured Instruction.

    Suggested Time
    🔗
    8 minutes
    🔗
    Objective
    🔗
    To name the parts of a linear programming model, using the tailor’s table.
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaways & Teacher Connection.

    1. Connecting to Learner Work: the cloth and machine rows of the table gave the two constraints in parts (a) and (b).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Each resource row gives one constraint; the “Available” column is its right-hand side.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The profit row gives the objective function \(P\text{,}\) which is not a constraint.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. A model is complete when it has the variables in words, every constraint (with \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\) and whole numbers where needed) and the objective with “maximise” or “minimise”.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 4.1.24 A Linear Programming Model.

    The unknowns \(x\) and \(y\) are the decision variables: the numbers we are free to choose. The quantity we want to make as large or as small as possible, written in terms of \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) is the objective function: \(P = ax + by\) for a profit, \(C = ax + by\) for a cost. A linear programming model is the decision variables, the constraints and the objective function, with the word “maximise” or “minimise”.
    🔗
    A resource table has one row for each resource, one column for each product and a column “Available”. Each resource row gives one constraint. The model is called linear because every expression in it has the form \(ax + by\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.26 Metal Boxes and Jikos.

    A jua kali workshop in Kamukunji makes metal boxes and jikos. A box needs \(6\) hours of welding and a jiko \(1\) hour; the welders have \(96\) hours a week. Each item needs \(1\) hour of painting, and the painter has \(26\) hours a week. The profit is Ksh \(450\) on a box and Ksh \(250\) on a jiko. Write a linear programming model for the week.
    🔗

    Solution.

    Let \(x\) be the number of metal boxes and \(y\) the number of jikos made in a week.
    🔗
    Maximise \(P = 450x + 250y\) subject to
    \begin{align*} 6x + y \amp \le 96\\ x + y \amp \le 26\\ x \ge 0,\quad y \amp \ge 0 \end{align*}
    with \(x\) and \(y\) whole numbers.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.28 A Night at Shimba Hills.

    A tour company in Mombasa must find beds for \(160\) tourists for a night at Shimba Hills. A banda sleeps \(7\) and costs Ksh \(9000\) a night; a dormitory block sleeps \(40\) and costs Ksh \(30\,000\) a night. Only \(2\) dormitory blocks are free, and the lodge has \(14\) caretakers, one for each banda or block that is booked. Write a model for the cheapest way to book the beds.
    🔗

    Solution.

    Let \(x\) be the number of bandas and \(y\) the number of dormitory blocks booked.
    🔗
    Beds for at least \(160\text{:}\) \(7x + 40y \ge 160\text{.}\) At most \(2\) dormitory blocks: \(y \le 2\text{.}\) One caretaker for each banda or block: \(x + y \le 14\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers.
    🔗
    Minimise \(C = 9000x + 30\,000y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In a cost problem the key constraint is usually “at least”: the company must give everyone a bed, and it wants to spend as little as it can while doing so.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.29 From a Model to Words.

    A farmer takes \(x\) crates of mangoes and \(y\) crates of oranges to market on a pickup. Her model is
    \begin{align*} x + y \amp \le 32\\ y \amp \ge 3x\\ P \amp = 50x + 80y \end{align*}
    with \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\) whole numbers. Say in words what each line means.
    🔗

    Solution.

    \(x + y \le 32\text{:}\) the pickup carries at most \(32\) crates.
    🔗
    \(y \ge 3x\text{:}\) she takes at least three times as many crates of oranges as crates of mangoes (with \(4\) crates of mangoes, at least \(12\) of oranges).
    🔗
    \(P = 50x + 80y\text{:}\) her profit in shillings, Ksh \(50\) on a crate of mangoes and Ksh \(80\) on a crate of oranges. It is the objective function, which she wants to maximise.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Exercises

    1.

    A candle maker in Nyeri makes boxes of plain candles and boxes of scented candles. A box of plain candles needs \(5\,\text{kg}\) of wax and \(1\) hour of work; a box of scented candles needs \(6\,\text{kg}\) of wax and \(7\) hours. Each week she has \(136\,\text{kg}\) of wax and \(120\) hours. The profit is Ksh \(300\) on a plain box and Ksh \(500\) on a scented box. Write the model.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Maximise \(P = 300x + 500y\) subject to \(5x + 6y \le 136\text{,}\) \(x + 7y \le 120\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the number of boxes of plain candles and \(y\) the number of boxes of scented candles made in a week. Wax: \(5x + 6y \le 136\text{.}\) Time: \(x + 7y \le 120\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers. Maximise \(P = 300x + 500y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    A greenhouse farmer in Naivasha grows tomatoes and capsicum in beds. A bed of tomatoes needs \(400\,\text{litres}\) of water and \(3\) hours of labour a week; a bed of capsicum needs \(700\,\text{litres}\) and \(8\) hours. She has \(7000\,\text{litres}\) of water and \(72\) hours of labour a week. The profit is Ksh \(2000\) a bed of tomatoes and Ksh \(4500\) a bed of capsicum. Write the model in its simplest form.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Maximise \(P = 2000x + 4500y\) subject to \(4x + 7y \le 70\text{,}\) \(3x + 8y \le 72\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the number of beds of tomatoes and \(y\) the number of beds of capsicum. Water: \(400x + 700y \le 7000\text{;}\) dividing by \(100\text{,}\) \(4x + 7y \le 70\text{.}\) Labour: \(3x + 8y \le 72\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers. Maximise \(P = 2000x + 4500y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    For a prize-giving day a school hires large tents that seat \(70\) at Ksh \(6000\) each and small tents that seat \(30\) at Ksh \(2400\) each. It needs at least \(350\) seats, and the field has room for at most \(10\) tents. Write a model for the cheapest choice.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Minimise \(C = 6000x + 2400y\) subject to \(7x + 3y \ge 35\text{,}\) \(x + y \le 10\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the number of large tents and \(y\) the number of small tents. Seats: \(70x + 30y \ge 350\text{;}\) dividing by \(10\text{,}\) \(7x + 3y \ge 35\text{.}\) Field: \(x + y \le 10\text{.}\) Also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) whole numbers. Minimise \(C = 6000x + 2400y\text{.}\)

    The tailor of Learner Experience 4.1.21 finds she has thread for at most \(40\) garments a week. Write the new constraint. Are the plans \((28, 6)\) and \((30, 5)\) still possible?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Check every constraint, old and new.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(x + y \le 40\text{;}\) both are still possible
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Thread: \(x + y \le 40\text{.}\) \((28, 6)\text{:}\) \(34 \le 40\text{,}\) and it already met the cloth and machine constraints, so it is still possible. \((30, 5)\text{:}\) \(35 \le 40\text{,}\) cloth \(125 \le 126\) and machine \(60 \le 64\text{,}\) so it is still possible. The new constraint does not change the best plan here.
