    Key Inquiry Questions 7.1.1.

    How do we use loci to decide where things can be placed or where they can move?
    🔗
    🔗
    A locus is the path a moving point can take, or the set of places where something is allowed to be. When a goat is tied to a peg, the places it can reach make a locus. When a county engineer wants a borehole to be fair to two villages, the places that are the same distance from both make a locus.
    🔗
    In this chapter you describe loci in words, construct them with a ruler and a pair of compasses, and combine them to find the places that obey several rules at once: a borehole sited fairly between two villages, the area a security guard’s floodlight covers, the angle a goalkeeper has to defend. The constructions are the ones you met in Grade 11; what is new is reading each one as a set of points that obey a rule.
    🔗
    Upon completion of this lesson, learners will be able to:
    1. Know: a locus is every point that obeys a rule and no other point; the basic loci in locus language and in construction language; every point on the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\) is equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) and every such point is on it.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Do: construct the perpendicular bisector of a segment; decide whether a grid point is on it, nearer to \(A\) or nearer to \(B\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Apply: place an object fairly between two others, using a scale drawing.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Teacher Resource 7.1.2 Problem-Solving and Discovery.

    Suggested Time
    🔗
    14 minutes
    🔗
    Objective
    🔗
    Learners find many points equidistant from two pegs and see that they form a straight line, and why the string must be longer than half the gap.
    🔗
    Teacher’s Role
    🔗
    Groups of four on the field with two pegs \(6\,\text{m}\) apart and two strings of equal length. If outside work is not possible, use paper with a scale of \(1\,\text{cm}\) to \(1\,\text{m}\) and a pair of compasses for the strings. Do not name “perpendicular bisector” until part (e). After part (d), ask: “Why did the \(2.5\,\text{m}\) strings never meet?”
    🔗
    🔗

    Learner Experience 7.1.3 Two Pegs on the Field.

    Two pegs \(A\) and \(B\) are pushed into the school field \(6\,\text{m}\) apart. Two learners each hold a string of the same length, one from \(A\) and one from \(B\text{,}\) and pull them tight. A third learner marks with chalk where the ends of the strings meet.
    🔗
    1. Use strings \(5\,\text{m}\) long. Where can the strings meet? How far is each meeting point from the middle \(M\) of \(AB\text{?}\)
      🔗
      Solution.
      At two points, one on each side of \(AB\text{.}\) Each is \(\sqrt{5^2 - 3^2} = 4\,\text{m}\) from \(M\) (a \(3, 4, 5\) triangle).
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Repeat with strings \(4\,\text{m}\) long.
      🔗
      Solution.
      Two points, each \(\sqrt{4^2 - 3^2} = \sqrt{7} \approx 2.6\,\text{m}\) from \(M\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Repeat with strings \(3\,\text{m}\) long.
      🔗
      Solution.
      Only one point: the strings meet at \(M\) itself.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Repeat with strings \(2.5\,\text{m}\) long.
      🔗
      Solution.
      They do not meet: \(2.5\,\text{m}\) is less than half of \(6\,\text{m}\text{,}\) so the two strings together cannot reach across \(AB\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Mark all your points with chalk. What shape do they make, and how does it cross \(AB\text{?}\)
      🔗
      Solution.
      A straight line through \(M\text{,}\) at right angles to \(AB\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

      Note 7.1.4.

      This is the moment to name the line, in two languages: in locus language it is “the points equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\)”; in construction language it is “the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\)”.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Teacher Resource 7.1.5 Structured Instruction.

    Suggested Time
    🔗
    8 minutes
    🔗
    Objective
    🔗
    To formalise the idea of a locus, the two languages for loci, and the perpendicular bisector locus.
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaways & Teacher Connection.

    1. Connecting to Learner Work: every chalk mark was the same distance from both pegs, and every point the same distance from both pegs could have been marked: that is what makes the line a locus.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The table of locus language and construction language is the map of the whole chapter.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The string length is the compass radius. It must be more than half of \(AB\text{,}\) or the arcs do not meet.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The construction works because each crossing point is one radius from \(A\) and the same radius from \(B\text{.}\) Recall the Grade 11 circumcircle construction, which used two perpendicular bisectors.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 7.1.6 Loci in Two Languages.

    A locus (plural loci) is the set of all points, and only those points, that obey a rule. We often say “the locus of a point \(P\) that moves so that ...”. Every point on the locus obeys the rule, and every point that obeys the rule is on the locus. A locus may be a line, a curve or a region. Points that are the same distance from two things are equidistant from them.
    🔗
    In this chapter “construct” means using only a ruler (as a straight edge) and a pair of compasses; “draw” allows measuring with a ruler and a protractor. Never rub out construction arcs. Measure lengths on drawings to the nearest millimetre and angles to the nearest degree.
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 7.1.8 The Perpendicular Bisector Locus.

    The locus of points equidistant from two points \(A\) and \(B\) is the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\text{:}\) the line through the midpoint of \(AB\) at right angles to \(AB\text{.}\) Both halves hold: every point on the perpendicular bisector is equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) and every point equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\) is on it.
    🔗
    To construct it:
    🔗
    1. Open the compasses to more than half of \(AB\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. With the point at \(A\text{,}\) draw an arc above \(AB\) and an arc below it.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Without changing the compasses, do the same from \(B\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Join the two points where the arcs cross. Keep the arcs.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.1.12 Constructing the Perpendicular Bisector.

    Draw a line segment \(AB = 7\,\text{cm}\) and construct its perpendicular bisector, with the compasses opened to \(5\,\text{cm}\text{.}\) How far from the midpoint \(M\) are the points where the arcs cross? Would a radius of \(3\,\text{cm}\) work?
    🔗

    Solution.

    Step 1: Draw \(AB = 7\,\text{cm}\text{.}\) Open the compasses to \(5\,\text{cm}\text{,}\) which is more than half of \(AB\) (\(3.5\,\text{cm}\)).
    🔗
    Step 2: With the point at \(A\text{,}\) draw an arc above \(AB\) and an arc below. Without changing the compasses, do the same from \(B\text{.}\) Join the two crossing points (Figure 7.1.9).
    🔗
    Check: each crossing point is \(5\,\text{cm}\) from \(A\) and \(3.5\,\text{cm}\) along from \(A\) to \(M\text{,}\) so by Pythagoras its distance from \(M\) is
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{5^2 - 3.5^2} = \sqrt{12.75} \approx 3.6\,\text{cm}. \end{equation*}
    Measure it on your drawing. Any radius more than \(3.5\,\text{cm}\) works. A radius of \(3\,\text{cm}\) does not: the arcs from \(A\) and \(B\) would not meet.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.1.13 On the Locus, or Nearer to \(A\) or \(B\text{?}\)

    \(A\) is \((1, 2)\) and \(B\) is \((7, 6)\text{.}\) Find the midpoint \(M\) of \(AB\text{.}\) For each of \(P(2, 7)\text{,}\) \(Q(6, 1)\) and \(R(5, 5)\text{,}\) decide whether it is on the locus of points equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) nearer to \(A\text{,}\) or nearer to \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    \(M = \left(\frac{1 + 7}{2}, \frac{2 + 6}{2}\right) = (4, 4)\text{.}\) Compare the squares of the distances, worked out by Pythagoras:
    \begin{align*} PA^2 \amp = 1^2 + 5^2 = 26, \amp PB^2 \amp = 5^2 + 1^2 = 26\\ QA^2 \amp = 5^2 + 1^2 = 26, \amp QB^2 \amp = 1^2 + 5^2 = 26\\ RA^2 \amp = 4^2 + 3^2 = 25, \amp RB^2 \amp = 2^2 + 1^2 = 5 \end{align*}
    So \(P\) and \(Q\) are on the locus, and \(R\) is nearer to \(B\text{.}\) Comparing squares means nothing has to be rounded. Figure 7.1.14 shows the points.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.1.15 A Water Tank Between Two Homes.

    Two homes \(H\) and \(K\) are \(80\,\text{m}\) apart beside a straight path that joins them. A water tank is to be the same distance from both homes and \(30\,\text{m}\) from the path. Make a scale drawing with \(1\,\text{cm}\) representing \(10\,\text{m}\text{,}\) and find how far the tank is from each home.
    🔗

    Solution.

    Step 1: Draw \(HK = 8\,\text{cm}\) and construct its perpendicular bisector. The tank is on this line, because it is equidistant from \(H\) and \(K\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: Mark the tank \(T\) on the bisector \(3\,\text{cm}\) from \(HK\text{.}\) Measure \(TH\text{:}\) about \(5.0\,\text{cm}\text{,}\) which represents \(50\,\text{m}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Check: the midpoint of \(HK\) is \(40\,\text{m}\) from each home, so \(TH = \sqrt{40^2 + 30^2} = 50\,\text{m}\text{.}\) The tank is \(50\,\text{m}\) from each home. (There is a second possible place, \(30\,\text{m}\) on the other side of the path.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 7.1.16 Loci with Geometry Software.

    Free geometry software such as GeoGebra lets you place two points \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) construct the perpendicular bisector with one tool, then put a point on it and drag it while the software shows \(PA\) and \(PB\text{.}\) Try it on a phone or computer: the two lengths stay equal however far you drag. Checking a construction this way is a useful digital skill.

    Exercises Exercises

    1.

    Draw a line segment \(AB = 9\,\text{cm}\) and construct its perpendicular bisector with the compasses opened to \(6\,\text{cm}\text{.}\) How far from the midpoint \(M\) are the points where your arcs cross?
    🔗
    Answer.
    About \(4.0\,\text{cm}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Each crossing point is \(6\,\text{cm}\) from \(A\text{,}\) and \(AM = 4.5\,\text{cm}\text{,}\) so its distance from \(M\) is \(\sqrt{6^2 - 4.5^2} = \sqrt{15.75} \approx 3.97\,\text{cm}\text{,}\) about \(4.0\,\text{cm}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    \(A\) is \((-2, 1)\) and \(B\) is \((4, 3)\text{.}\) Find the midpoint \(M\) of \(AB\text{.}\) For each of \(P(0, 5)\text{,}\) \(Q(2, -1)\) and \(R(3, 1)\text{,}\) decide whether it is on the locus of points equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) nearer to \(A\text{,}\) or nearer to \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(M(1, 2)\text{;}\) \(P\) and \(Q\) are on the locus; \(R\) is nearer to \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(PA^2 = 4 + 16 = 20\text{,}\) \(PB^2 = 16 + 4 = 20\text{:}\) on. \(QA^2 = 16 + 4 = 20\text{,}\) \(QB^2 = 4 + 16 = 20\text{:}\) on. \(RA^2 = 25 + 0 = 25\text{,}\) \(RB^2 = 1 + 4 = 5\text{:}\) nearer to \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Two matatu stages \(S\) and \(T\) are \(1.2\,\text{km}\) apart on a straight road. A kiosk must be equidistant from \(S\) and \(T\) and \(0.8\,\text{km}\) from the road. Make a scale drawing with \(1\,\text{cm}\) representing \(0.2\,\text{km}\text{.}\) How far is the kiosk from each stage?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(1.0\,\text{km}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Draw \(ST = 6\,\text{cm}\) and construct its perpendicular bisector. Mark the kiosk on it \(4\,\text{cm}\) from \(ST\text{.}\) It measures \(5\,\text{cm}\) from each stage, which is \(1.0\,\text{km}\text{.}\) Check: \(\sqrt{0.6^2 + 0.8^2} = 1.0\,\text{km}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    \(P\) is on the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\text{,}\) \(5\,\text{cm}\) from \(AB\text{,}\) and \(PA = 13\,\text{cm}\text{.}\) Find the length of \(AB\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(24\,\text{cm}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    If \(M\) is the midpoint of \(AB\text{,}\) triangle \(PMA\) has a right angle at \(M\text{,}\) so \(AM = \sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = 12\,\text{cm}\) and \(AB = 24\,\text{cm}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Write each locus in construction language.
    🔗
    1. The locus of a point that is always as far from the gate \(G\) as from the tap \(T\text{.}\)

    2. The locus of a point \(2\,\text{m}\) from the tap \(T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The locus of a point equidistant from two parallel fences.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) The perpendicular bisector of \(GT\text{.}\) (b) A circle, centre \(T\text{,}\) radius \(2\,\text{m}\text{.}\) (c) The line parallel to both fences, halfway between them.

    6. Challenge: Why the Bisector Works.

    \(P\) is equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) and \(M\) is the midpoint of \(AB\text{.}\) Show that \(PM\) is at right angles to \(AB\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compare triangles \(PMA\) and \(PMB\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The triangles are congruent (SSS), so the angles at \(M\) are equal, and each is \(90^{\circ}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    In triangles \(PMA\) and \(PMB\text{:}\) \(PA = PB\) (given), \(AM = MB\) (\(M\) is the midpoint) and \(PM\) is common. So the triangles are congruent (SSS), and angle \(PMA\) equals angle \(PMB\text{.}\) These two angles lie on the straight line \(AB\text{,}\) so they add to \(180^{\circ}\text{,}\) and each is \(90^{\circ}\text{.}\)
