    Subsection 9.2.1 Drawing Trigonometric Graphs

    Learner Experience 9.2.1 Doubling the Sine.

    1. Copy and complete a table of values of \(y = \sin x\) and \(y = 2\sin x\) for \(x = 0^\circ, 30^\circ, 60^\circ, \ldots, 360^\circ\text{.}\) Give values to 2 decimal places.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Plot both graphs on one grid and join each set of points with a smooth curve.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Write down the greatest and least values of each graph. After what length along the \(x\)-axis does each graph repeat itself?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Predict how the graph of \(y = \sin 3x\) differs from \(y = \sin x\text{.}\) Check with a table at steps of \(15^\circ\) from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 9.2.2 Drawing a Trigonometric Graph.

    To draw \(y = a\sin(bx + c) + d\) or \(y = a\cos(bx + c) + d\text{:}\)
    1. choose a step for \(x\text{,}\) such as \(30^\circ\) or \(15^\circ\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. for each \(x\) work out the angle \(bx + c\text{,}\) take its sine or cosine (calculator in degree mode), multiply by \(a\) and add \(d\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. plot the points and join them with a smooth curve.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The greatest height of the wave above its middle is its amplitude; the length along the axis after which it repeats is its period. Both are defined fully in Subsection 9.2.2.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.2.4 A Cosine Table.

    Make a table of values of \(y = 2.5\cos x\) for \(0^\circ \le x \le 360^\circ\) at steps of \(30^\circ\text{.}\) State the amplitude and the period.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(x\) \(0^\circ\) \(30^\circ\) \(60^\circ\) \(90^\circ\) \(120^\circ\) \(150^\circ\) \(180^\circ\) \(210^\circ\) \(240^\circ\) \(270^\circ\) \(300^\circ\) \(330^\circ\) \(360^\circ\)
    \(2.5\cos x\) \(2.5\) \(2.17\) \(1.25\) \(0\) \(-1.25\) \(-2.17\) \(-2.5\) \(-2.17\) \(-1.25\) \(0\) \(1.25\) \(2.17\) \(2.5\)
    🔗
    The graph rises to 2.5 and falls to \(-2.5\text{:}\) the amplitude is 2.5. It repeats after \(360^\circ\text{:}\) the period is \(360^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.2.5 A Table with a Phase Angle.

    Make a table of values of \(y = 2\sin(x + 30^\circ)\) for \(0^\circ \le x \le 360^\circ\) at steps of \(30^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Work out \(x + 30^\circ\) first (from \(30^\circ\) to \(390^\circ\)), then its sine, then multiply by 2.
    🔗
    \(x\) \(0^\circ\) \(30^\circ\) \(60^\circ\) \(90^\circ\) \(120^\circ\) \(150^\circ\) \(180^\circ\) \(210^\circ\) \(240^\circ\) \(270^\circ\) \(300^\circ\) \(330^\circ\) \(360^\circ\)
    \(x + 30^\circ\) \(30\) \(60\) \(90\) \(120\) \(150\) \(180\) \(210\) \(240\) \(270\) \(300\) \(330\) \(360\) \(390\)
    \(2\sin(x + 30^\circ)\) \(1\) \(1.73\) \(2\) \(1.73\) \(1\) \(0\) \(-1\) \(-1.73\) \(-2\) \(-1.73\) \(-1\) \(0\) \(1\)
    🔗
    The values are those of \(2\sin x\) moved one column to the left: the curve is the \(2\sin x\) curve moved \(30^\circ\) to the left. This is made exact in Subsection 9.2.2 and Subsection 9.4.2.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.2.6 Solving an Equation with Two Graphs.

    Plot \(y = \sin 2x\) and \(y = \sin x\) for \(0^\circ \le x \le 360^\circ\) on one grid and use the graphs to solve \(\sin 2x = \sin x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(x\) \(0^\circ\) \(30^\circ\) \(60^\circ\) \(90^\circ\) \(120^\circ\) \(150^\circ\) \(180^\circ\) \(210^\circ\) \(240^\circ\) \(270^\circ\) \(300^\circ\) \(330^\circ\) \(360^\circ\)
    \(\sin 2x\) \(0\) \(0.87\) \(0.87\) \(0\) \(-0.87\) \(-0.87\) \(0\) \(0.87\) \(0.87\) \(0\) \(-0.87\) \(-0.87\) \(0\)
    \(\sin x\) \(0\) \(0.5\) \(0.87\) \(1\) \(0.87\) \(0.5\) \(0\) \(-0.5\) \(-0.87\) \(-1\) \(-0.87\) \(-0.5\) \(0\)
    🔗
    The graphs (Figure 9.2.7) meet at \(x = 0^\circ\text{,}\) \(60^\circ\text{,}\) \(180^\circ\text{,}\) \(300^\circ\) and \(360^\circ\text{.}\) These are the solutions of \(\sin 2x = \sin x\) in \(0^\circ \le x \le 360^\circ\text{.}\) The graphs give the solutions; Section 9.3 finds solutions like these by algebra.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Warning 9.2.8 Degree Mode.

    In radian mode a calculator gives \(\sin 30 = -0.99\text{.}\) Check that \(\sin 30 = 0.5\) before you start every table.

    Subsection 9.2.2 Amplitude, Period, Wavelength and Phase Angle

    Learner Experience 9.2.12 Reading a Wave.

    Part A: A Wave on the Board.

    Your teacher shows the graph of \(y = 3\sin(x + 50^\circ)\) for \(-180^\circ \le x \le 360^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    1. Write down the greatest and least values of \(y\text{,}\) and half their difference.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. How far along the \(x\)-axis is one complete wave?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Where does the wave cross its middle line going up? Compare with \(y = \sin x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Part B. In the interactive in Figure 9.2.15, set \(a = 2\text{,}\) \(b = 1\) and \(c = 0\text{.}\) Now change only \(b\text{,}\) to 2, 3 and 4, and record the period each time.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Change only \(c\text{.}\) What happens to the wave?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 9.2.13 Features of a Wave.

    For \(y = a\sin(bx + c) + d\) or \(y = a\cos(bx + c) + d\text{,}\) with \(b \gt 0\) and \(x\) in degrees:
    🔗
    • the mean line is \(y = d\text{,}\) halfway between the greatest and least values;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the amplitude is \(|a| = \dfrac{\text{greatest} - \text{least}}{2}\text{:}\) how far the wave rises above its mean line;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the period is \(\dfrac{360^\circ}{b}\text{:}\) how far along the axis before the wave repeats itself;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the wavelength is the length of one complete wave measured along the horizontal axis, for example from one crest to the next. For a graph drawn against an angle in degrees, period and wavelength are the same number of degrees. For a real wave drawn against time, the repeat length is a period (in seconds or hours); drawn against distance, it is a wavelength (in metres or km);
      🔗
      🔗
    • the phase angle is \(c\text{,}\) the angle added inside the bracket, which measures how far ahead of \(y = a\sin bx\) the wave starts, in degrees of the angle \(bx + c\text{.}\) Along the \(x\)-axis the lead is \(\dfrac{c}{b}\text{:}\) since \(bx + c = b\left(x + \dfrac{c}{b}\right)\text{,}\) the graph is that of \(y = a\sin bx\) moved \(\dfrac{c}{b}\) to the left (to the right when \(c \lt 0\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.2.16 Amplitude and Period from an Equation.

    Find the amplitude and the period of \(y = 5\sin 3x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The amplitude is \(5\text{.}\) The period is \(360^\circ \div 3 = 120^\circ\text{:}\) three complete waves fit into \(360^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.2.17 A Negative Multiplier.

    Find the amplitude and the period of \(y = -2\cos\dfrac{x}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The amplitude is \(2\text{:}\) a distance is never negative, and the minus sign only turns the wave upside down. Here \(b = \dfrac{1}{2}\text{,}\) so the period is \(360^\circ \div \dfrac{1}{2} = 720^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.2.18 Reading a Graph.

    Figure 9.2.19 shows a wave of the form \(y = a\sin(x + c) + d\text{.}\) Find its amplitude, mean line, wavelength and phase angle, and write its equation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The greatest value is \(4\) and the least is \(-2\text{.}\) The amplitude is \(\dfrac{4 - (-2)}{2} = 3\) and the mean line is \(y = \dfrac{4 + (-2)}{2} = 1\text{.}\) One complete wave is \(360^\circ\) long.
    🔗
    The wave rises through its mean line at \(x = 30^\circ\text{,}\) where \(y = \sin x\) would rise through its mean line at \(0^\circ\text{.}\) So the wave is \(30^\circ\) behind: the phase angle is \(-30^\circ\text{,}\) and \(y = 3\sin(x - 30^\circ) + 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.2.20 Phase Angle and Translation.

    For \(y = 4\sin(3x + 45^\circ)\text{,}\) find the amplitude, the period and the phase angle, and describe how the graph is related to \(y = 4\sin 3x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The amplitude is \(4\text{,}\) the period \(360^\circ \div 3 = 120^\circ\) and the phase angle \(45^\circ\text{.}\) Since \(3x + 45^\circ = 3(x + 15^\circ)\text{,}\) the graph is \(y = 4\sin 3x\) moved \(15^\circ\) to the left.

    Example 9.2.21 Tides at Mombasa.

    A simple model of the depth of water at a jetty in Kilindini harbour, Mombasa, is \(h = 2 + 1.5\sin(30t)^\circ\text{,}\) where \(h\) is the depth in metres and \(t\) is the time in hours after midnight, \(0 \le t \le 24\text{.}\) Find the greatest and least depths, when the water is deepest, and the period.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The sine is at most 1 and at least \(-1\text{,}\) so the greatest depth is \(2 + 1.5 = 3.5\,\text{m}\) and the least is \(2 - 1.5 = 0.5\,\text{m}\text{.}\) The water is deepest when \(30t = 90\) or \(450\text{,}\) that is at \(t = 3\) and \(t = 15\) (3 a.m. and 3 p.m.).
    🔗
    The period is \(360 \div 30 = 12\) hours: two high tides a day (Figure 9.2.22). The graph is drawn against time, so its repeat length is a period, not a wavelength.

    Note 9.2.23 Tsunami Waves.

    Far out at sea a tsunami has a wavelength of hundreds of kilometres and a height of less than a metre, so ships hardly notice it. Near the shore the wave slows down and grows much taller. The Indian Ocean tsunami of December 2004 reached the Kenyan coast. If you are at the coast and the sea suddenly draws back, move at once to high ground and stay there until it is safe.
