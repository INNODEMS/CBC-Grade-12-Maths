    Subsection 6.2.1 Deriving the Trapezoidal Rule

    Learner Experience 6.2.1 Trapezia Under a Curve.

    The region under the curve \(y = x^2 + 2x + 3\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 4\) is cut into four strips, each \(1\) unit wide. In each strip the curve is replaced by a straight line joining its two ends, so each strip becomes a trapezium.
    🔗
    1. Copy and complete the table of values of \(y = x^2 + 2x + 3\) for \(x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the area of each strip as a trapezium.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Add the four areas to estimate the area of the region.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Work in pairs. Call the strip width \(h\) and the heights \(y_0, y_1, y_2, y_3, y_4\text{.}\) Write the area of each trapezium using these letters, add the four areas and collect the terms. Which heights appear twice?
      🔗
      🔗
    5. The exact area of the region is \(49\tfrac{1}{3}\text{.}\) Use the interactive in Figure 6.2.4 with \(8\) strips. Is the estimate closer?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 6.2.3 Deriving the Trapezoidal Rule.

    To estimate the area bounded by a curve, the \(x\)-axis and the lines \(x = a\) and \(x = b\text{,}\) cut the region into \(n\) strips of equal width
    \begin{equation*} h = \frac{b - a}{n}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The heights of the curve at the edges of the strips are the ordinates \(y_0, y_1, \ldots, y_n\text{,}\) at \(x_0 = a\text{,}\) \(x_1 = a + h\text{,}\) and so on up to \(x_n = b\text{.}\) There are \(n + 1\) ordinates: the strips are the gaps between them.
    🔗
    In general, strip \(k\) lies between the ordinates \(y_{k-1}\) and \(y_k\text{.}\) Replace the curve over the strip by the straight line, or chord, joining its two ends. The strip becomes a trapezium of area \(\tfrac{1}{2}h(y_{k-1} + y_k)\text{.}\) Adding the \(n\) trapezia,
    \begin{align*} A \amp \approx \tfrac{1}{2}h(y_0 + y_1) + \tfrac{1}{2}h(y_1 + y_2) + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{2}h(y_{n-1} + y_n)\\ \amp = \tfrac{1}{2}h\,[y_0 + y_1 + y_1 + y_2 + \cdots + y_{n-1} + y_{n-1} + y_n]\\ \amp = \tfrac{1}{2}h\,[y_0 + y_n + 2(y_1 + y_2 + \cdots + y_{n-1})]\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    This is the trapezoidal rule: half the strip width times the first plus the last plus twice the rest. Each inner ordinate is a side of two trapezia, so it is counted twice.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.2.5 Offsets From a Fence.

    Wanjiku measured a plot beside a road near Nyeri. Along a straight fence \(60\,\text{m}\) long she measured the distance from the fence to the curved edge of the plot (the offset) every \(10\,\text{m}\text{.}\) Use the trapezoidal rule to estimate the area of the plot.
    🔗
    Solution.
    There are \(7\) offsets, so there are \(6\) strips, each \(h = 10\,\text{m}\) wide. The first and last offsets are \(32\) and \(24\text{;}\) the inner ones add to \(35 + 37 + 36 + 33 + 29 = 170\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} A \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times [32 + 24 + 2 \times 170] = 5 \times 396 = 1980 \end{equation*}
    The plot is about \(1980\,\text{m}^2\text{.}\) Note that \(7\) measurements make \(6\) strips.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.2.7 Strips Two Units Wide.

    Use the trapezoidal rule with \(3\) strips to estimate the area bounded by the curve \(y = 8 + 2x - \tfrac{1}{2}x^2\text{,}\) the \(x\)-axis and the lines \(x = 0\) and \(x = 6\text{.}\) Then repeat with \(6\) strips.
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(3\) strips, \(h = (6 - 0) \div 3 = 2\text{,}\) so the ordinates are at \(x = 0, 2, 4, 6\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times [8 + 2 + 2(10 + 8)] = 1 \times 46 = 46 \end{equation*}
    With \(6\) strips, \(h = 1\) and the ordinates at \(x = 0, 1, \ldots, 6\) are \(8, 9.5, 10, 9.5, 8, 5.5, 2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1 \times [8 + 2 + 2(9.5 + 10 + 9.5 + 8 + 5.5)] = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 95 = 47.5 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.2.9 A Weight Column.

    The width of a flower bed was measured every \(2\,\text{m}\) along a straight path. The widths, in metres, were \(3.0, 4.2, 4.8, 4.6, 3.8, 2.4\text{.}\) Estimate the area of the flower bed with the trapezoidal rule.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Give each width a “weight”: \(1\) for the first and last, \(2\) for the others. Then add the products.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 40.2 = 40.2 \end{equation*}
    The flower bed is about \(40.2\,\text{m}^2\text{.}\) The weight column is the trapezoidal rule written as a table; it is also how a spreadsheet does the calculation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Type decimals with a point, for example 37.6. Give exact answers unless the question asks you to round.

    Exercises Exercises

    1.

    The offsets from a straight hedge to the edge of a vegetable garden, measured every \(5\,\text{m}\text{,}\) are \(6, 8, 9, 8, 5\) metres. Use the trapezoidal rule to estimate the area of the garden.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(152.5\,\text{m}^2\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(5\) offsets make \(4\) strips with \(h = 5\text{.}\) \(A \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 5 \times [6 + 5 + 2(8 + 9 + 8)] = 2.5 \times 61 = 152.5\,\text{m}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Use the trapezoidal rule with strips \(0.5\) wide to estimate the area bounded by \(y = 12 + 3x - 2x^2\text{,}\) the \(x\)-axis and the lines \(x = 0\) and \(x = 3\text{.}\) The exact area is \(31.5\text{.}\) Is your estimate too big or too small?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(31.25\text{;}\) too small
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(6\) strips. The ordinates at \(x = 0, 0.5, \ldots, 3\) are \(12, 13, 13, 12, 10, 7, 3\text{.}\) \(A \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.5 \times [12 + 3 + 2(13 + 13 + 12 + 10 + 7)] = 0.25 \times 125 = 31.25\text{,}\) which is less than \(31.5\text{:}\) the curve bends downwards, so the chords lie below it.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    The width of a fish pond was measured every \(3\,\text{m}\) along one straight side: \(4, 6, 7, 7, 6, 5, 3\) metres. How many strips do these measurements make? How long is the straight side? Estimate the area of the pond.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(6\) strips; \(18\,\text{m}\text{;}\) \(103.5\,\text{m}^2\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(7\) measurements make \(6\) strips, so the side is \(6 \times 3 = 18\,\text{m}\) long. \(A \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 3 \times [4 + 3 + 2(6 + 7 + 7 + 6 + 5)] = 1.5 \times 69 = 103.5\,\text{m}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Offsets measured every \(5\,\text{m}\) along a fence are \(10, 14, p, 15, 11\) metres. The trapezoidal estimate of the area is \(277.5\,\text{m}^2\text{.}\) Find \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(p = 16\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 5 \times [10 + 11 + 2(14 + p + 15)] = 277.5\text{,}\) so \(21 + 58 + 2p = 111\text{,}\) \(2p = 32\) and \(p = 16\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    A region is cut into three strips of width \(h\) with ordinates \(y_0, y_1, y_2, y_3\text{.}\) Add the areas of the three trapezia and show that the total is \(\tfrac{1}{2}h[y_0 + y_3 + 2(y_1 + y_2)]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Shown.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\tfrac{1}{2}h(y_0 + y_1) + \tfrac{1}{2}h(y_1 + y_2) + \tfrac{1}{2}h(y_2 + y_3) = \tfrac{1}{2}h(y_0 + 2y_1 + 2y_2 + y_3) = \tfrac{1}{2}h[y_0 + y_3 + 2(y_1 + y_2)]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Challenge: A Straight Line.

    Use the trapezoidal rule to estimate the area bounded by \(y = 3x + 1\text{,}\) the \(x\)-axis and the lines \(x = 0\) and \(x = 4\text{,}\) with \(1\text{,}\) \(2\) and \(4\) strips. Explain your results.
    🔗
    Hint.
    What shape is the region?
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(28\) each time
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(1\) strip: \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times (1 + 13) = 28\text{.}\) \(2\) strips: \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times [1 + 13 + 2 \times 7] = 28\text{.}\) \(4\) strips: \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1 \times [1 + 13 + 2(4 + 7 + 10)] = 28\text{.}\) The region is itself a trapezium of area \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times (1 + 13) = 28\text{:}\) every chord lies along the line, so the rule is exact however many strips are used.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.2.2 Rivers, Fields and the Size of the Error

    Learner Experience 6.2.14 Across the River.

    A water engineering team measures the River Tana near Garissa. They stretch a rope \(24\,\text{m}\) across the river from bank to bank and measure the depth every \(4\,\text{m}\text{.}\) The depths are \(0, 1.2, 2.0, 2.6, 2.2, 1.4, 0\) metres. (The measurements are made up for this activity.)
    🔗
    1. How many strips do the measurements make, and how wide is each strip?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Use the trapezoidal rule to estimate the area of the cross-section.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The water flows at \(0.5\,\text{m/s}\text{.}\) How many cubic metres of water pass the rope each second, and each minute?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The river bed curves smoothly like a bowl between the measured points. Is your estimate of the area too big or too small?
      🔗
      🔗
    5. How could the team improve the estimate?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 6.2.16 Too Big or Too Small?

    Look at where the chords lie compared with the curve (Figure 6.2.17).
    • If the curve bends upwards, like a bowl, the chords lie above it, so the trapezia include extra area: the trapezoidal rule overestimates.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If the curve bends downwards, like an arch, the chords lie below it, so some area is left out: the rule underestimates.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If the graph is a straight line, each chord lies along it and the rule is exact.
      🔗
      🔗
    In every case, narrower strips hug the curve more closely and make the error smaller.
    🔗

    Example 6.2.18 A Drainage Ditch at Ahero.

    A drainage ditch at the Ahero rice scheme is \(3\,\text{m}\) wide. Its depth, measured every \(0.5\,\text{m}\) across, is \(0, 0.42, 0.66, 0.72, 0.62, 0.38, 0\) metres. Estimate the area of the cross-section, and the volume of soil dug out to make \(50\,\text{m}\) of ditch.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(7\) depths make \(6\) strips with \(h = 0.5\text{.}\) The inner depths add to \(0.42 + 0.66 + 0.72 + 0.62 + 0.38 = 2.8\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} A \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.5 \times [0 + 0 + 2 \times 2.8] = 0.25 \times 5.6 = 1.4 \end{equation*}
    The cross-section is about \(1.4\,\text{m}^2\text{.}\) A ditch \(50\,\text{m}\) long is a prism, so the volume of soil is about \(1.4 \times 50 = 70\,\text{m}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.2.19 An Irregular Field.

    A surveyor lays a straight base line \(80\,\text{m}\) long across an irregular field and measures offsets to the boundary on both sides every \(20\,\text{m}\) (Figure 6.2.20). Estimate the area of the field. Land here sells at Ksh \(2\,500\,000\) per hectare (\(1\,\text{ha} = 10\,000\,\text{m}^2\)). Estimate the value of the field.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Treat each side of the base line separately; each has \(5\) offsets, so \(4\) strips with \(h = 20\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \text{upper side} \amp \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 20 \times [10 + 8 + 2(18 + 25 + 21)] = 10 \times 146 = 1460\\ \text{lower side} \amp \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 20 \times [6 + 4 + 2(12 + 16 + 14)] = 10 \times 94 = 940 \end{align*}
    The field is about \(1460 + 940 = 2400\,\text{m}^2 = 0.24\,\text{ha}\text{.}\) Its value is about \(0.24 \times 2\,500\,000 = 600\,000\text{,}\) that is Ksh \(600\,000\text{.}\)

    Example 6.2.21 Which Way Is the Error?

    1. In Learner Experience 6.2.1 the trapezoidal estimate for \(y = x^2 + 2x + 3\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 4\) with \(4\) strips was \(50\text{.}\) The exact area is \(49\tfrac{1}{3}\text{.}\) Explain why the estimate is too big.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. In Example 6.2.7 the estimate for \(y = 8 + 2x - \tfrac{1}{2}x^2\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 6\) with \(3\) strips was \(46\text{.}\) The exact area is \(48\text{.}\) Explain why the estimate is too small.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(y = x^2 + 2x + 3\) bends upwards like a bowl, so each chord lies above the curve and every trapezium includes a little extra area: \(50 \gt 49\tfrac{1}{3}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(y = 8 + 2x - \tfrac{1}{2}x^2\) bends downwards like an arch, so each chord lies below the curve and every trapezium misses a little area: \(46 \lt 48\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    6. Challenge: More Soundings.

    The depth of a river \(20\,\text{m}\) wide is \(d = 0.02x(20 - x)\) metres at \(x\) metres from one bank. Estimate the area of the cross-section with soundings every \(5\,\text{m}\text{,}\) then every \(2.5\,\text{m}\text{.}\) The true area is \(26.67\,\text{m}^2\) (to 2 decimal places). What happens, and why?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compare the two errors.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(25\,\text{m}^2\) and \(26.25\,\text{m}^2\text{;}\) both too small, the second much closer
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Every \(5\,\text{m}\text{:}\) depths \(0, 1.5, 2, 1.5, 0\text{,}\) so \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 5 \times 2 \times 5 = 25\text{.}\) Every \(2.5\,\text{m}\text{:}\) depths \(0, 0.875, 1.5, 1.875, 2, 1.875, 1.5, 0.875, 0\text{,}\) so \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.5 \times 2 \times 10.5 = 26.25\text{.}\) Both are too small, because the bed is shaped like a bowl and the chords lie above it. The errors are about \(1.67\) and \(0.42\text{:}\) halving the strip width made the error about a quarter as big.
