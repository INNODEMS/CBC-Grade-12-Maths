    Learner Experience 10.2.1 Corners of a Box.

    A carton \(40\,\text{cm}\) long, \(30\,\text{cm}\) wide and \(20\,\text{cm}\) high has one corner at the origin \(O\text{.}\) It is lettered like the cuboid in Example 10.1.7: base \(OABC\) with \(A\) on the \(x\)-axis and \(C\) on the \(y\)-axis, and \(D\text{,}\) \(E\text{,}\) \(F\text{,}\) \(G\) above \(O\text{,}\) \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) \(C\text{.}\) Distances are in centimetres.
    🔗
    1. The position vector of a point is the column of moves from \(O\) to the point. Write the position vectors of \(A\text{,}\) \(B\) and \(F\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

      🔗

    Key Takeaway 10.2.3 Position Vectors and the Vector \(\overrightarrow{AB}\).

    The position vector of a point \(P\) is \(\overrightarrow{OP}\text{,}\) the journey from the origin to the point. We write it with the small bold letter, \(\mathbf{p}\text{.}\) The point \(P(x, y, z)\) has position vector \(\mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A vector such as \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) that is not tied to \(O\) is a free vector. Going from \(A\) to \(B\) is the same as going from \(A\) back to \(O\) and then from \(O\) to \(B\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a} \end{equation*}
    (end minus start). Going the other way, \(\overrightarrow{BA} = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} = -\overrightarrow{AB}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Two vectors are equal when all their components are equal: they have the same length and the same direction, wherever they are drawn.
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 10.2.5 Writing Vectors by Hand.

    Printed books show vectors in bold, \(\mathbf{a}\text{.}\) You cannot write bold by hand, so underline the letter (\(\underline{a}\)) or draw an arrow over the two letters, \(\overrightarrow{AB}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Warning 10.2.6 End Minus Start.

    \(\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\) is the vector from \(B\) to \(A\text{,}\) not from \(A\) to \(B\text{.}\) For \(\overrightarrow{AB}\text{,}\) start with the end point: \(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.2.8 Position Vectors of Points.

    (a) Write the position vector of \(A(3, -1, 4)\text{.}\) (b) The point \(P\) has position vector \(\mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 5 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) Write the coordinates of \(P\) and say which coordinate plane it lies in.
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) \(\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\text{:}\) the coordinates written as a column.
    🔗
    (b) \(P(0, 5, -2)\text{.}\) Its \(x\)-coordinate is \(0\text{,}\) so \(P\) lies in the \(yz\)-plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.2.9 The Vector Between Two Points.

    \(A\) is \((2, 5, -1)\) and \(B\) is \((6, 3, 4)\text{.}\) Find \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) and \(\overrightarrow{BA}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    End minus start:
    \begin{equation*} \overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    and \(\overrightarrow{BA} = -\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} -4 \\ 2 \\ -5 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.2.10 Finding a Point from a Vector.

    (a) \(A\) is \((1, -2, 3)\) and \(\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ -6 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) Find \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) \(ABCD\) is a parallelogram with \(A(1, 0, 2)\text{,}\) \(B(4, 1, 0)\) and \(C(6, 5, 3)\text{.}\) Find \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) \(\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a} + \overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ -6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) so \(B\) is \((5, 3, -3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) In the parallelogram \(ABCD\text{,}\) the sides \(AD\) and \(BC\) are opposite, so \(\overrightarrow{AD} = \overrightarrow{BC}\text{.}\) Now \(\overrightarrow{BC} = \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{d} = \mathbf{a} + \overrightarrow{AD} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    and \(D\) is \((3, 4, 5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    For the carton in Learner Experience 10.2.1, find \(\overrightarrow{OG}\) and \(\overrightarrow{EC}\text{.}\)

    \(\overrightarrow{OG} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 30 \\ 20 \end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) \(\overrightarrow{EC} = \begin{pmatrix} -40 \\ 30 \\ -20 \end{pmatrix}\)

    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\begin{pmatrix} 30 \\ 20 \\ 40 \end{pmatrix}\)

    5.

    \(\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(B\) is \((5, 2, 0)\text{.}\) Find \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(A(2, 3, -4)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{b} - \overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 - 3 \\ 2 + 1 \\ 0 - 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)

    6. Challenge: Where the Diagonals Meet.

    \(ABCD\) is a parallelogram with \(A(2, -1, 3)\text{,}\) \(B(5, 1, 4)\) and \(D(0, 2, 6)\text{.}\) Find \(C\text{.}\) Then show that the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) have the same midpoint.
    🔗
    Hint.
    \(\overrightarrow{BC} = \overrightarrow{AD}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(C(3, 4, 7)\text{;}\) both midpoints are \((2.5, 1.5, 5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\overrightarrow{AD} = \mathbf{d} - \mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{b} + \overrightarrow{AD} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) The midpoint of \(AC\) is \(\left(\frac{2 + 3}{2}, \frac{-1 + 4}{2}, \frac{3 + 7}{2}\right) = (2.5, 1.5, 5)\text{,}\) and the midpoint of \(BD\) is \(\left(\frac{5 + 0}{2}, \frac{1 + 2}{2}, \frac{4 + 6}{2}\right) = (2.5, 1.5, 5)\text{:}\) the same point.
