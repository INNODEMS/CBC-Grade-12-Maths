    Subsection 12.4.1 Range, Interquartile Range and Quartile Deviation

    Learner Experience 12.4.1 Two Matatu Routes.

    The times, in minutes, of \(11\) matatu journeys on each of two routes into Nairobi were recorded.
    🔗
    1. Put each route’s times in order. Find the range of each: the longest time minus the shortest.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the median and the quartiles of each route.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. For each route find \(Q_3 - Q_1\) and half of it.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. One Route A trip took \(45\) minutes because the matatu broke down. Which of your measures does this one trip affect?
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Which route is more reliable? Give a reason.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 12.4.3 Range, Interquartile Range and Quartile Deviation.

    The range is the largest value minus the smallest. The interquartile range (IQR) is \(Q_3 - Q_1\text{,}\) the spread of the middle half of the data, and the quartile deviation (QD, or semi-interquartile range) is half of it, \(\dfrac{Q_3 - Q_1}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The range depends on only the two most extreme values, so one unusual value can change it a lot. The IQR and QD ignore the top and bottom quarters, so they are not affected by extreme values.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12.4.4 M-Pesa Transactions.

    The numbers of M-Pesa transactions at an agent in Kisumu on \(15\) days were \(48, 55, 62, 41, 58, 66, 52, 45, 70, 60, 50, 57, 63, 44, 54\text{.}\) Find the range, the interquartile range and the quartile deviation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    In order: \(41, 44, 45, 48, 50, 52, 54, 55, 57, 58, 60, 62, 63, 66, 70\text{.}\) The range is \(70 - 41 = 29\text{.}\)
    🔗
    With \(n + 1 = 16\text{,}\) \(Q_1\) is the 4th value, \(48\text{;}\) the median is the 8th, \(55\text{;}\) \(Q_3\) is the 12th, \(62\text{.}\) So IQR \(= 62 - 48 = 14\) and QD \(= 7\) transactions.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12.4.5 Milk Sales: IQR by Calculation.

    The milk sold, in litres (to the nearest litre), on \(70\) days at a dairy kiosk in Nakuru is shown below. Calculate the interquartile range and the quartile deviation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The cumulative frequencies are \(5, 16, 32, 52, 64, 70\text{.}\) \(Q_1\) is at \(70 \div 4 = 17.5\text{,}\) in 30–39; \(Q_3\) at \(52.5\text{,}\) in 50–59.
    \begin{align*} Q_1 \amp = 29.5 + \frac{17.5 - 16}{16} \times 10 = 30.44\ldots\\ Q_3 \amp = 49.5 + \frac{52.5 - 52}{12} \times 10 = 49.92\ldots \end{align*}
    Keeping full accuracy, IQR \(= 49.92 - 30.44 = 19.48 \approx 19.5\,\text{litres}\) and QD \(= 9.74 \approx 9.7\,\text{litres}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12.4.7 Milk Sales: IQR from the Curve.

    The milk sold, in litres (to the nearest litre), on \(70\) days at a dairy kiosk in Nakuru has frequencies \(5, 11, 16, 20, 12, 6\) for the classes 10–19 to 60–69. Use the cumulative frequency curve to estimate the interquartile range and the quartile deviation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Plot \((9.5, 0)\) and the cumulative frequencies \(5, 16, 32, 52, 64, 70\) at the upper class boundaries. Go across from \(70 \div 4 = 17.5\) and from \(52.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(Q_1 \approx 30.4\,\text{litres}\) and \(Q_3 \approx 49.9\,\text{litres}\text{,}\) so the IQR is about \(19.5\,\text{litres}\) and the quartile deviation about \(9.7\,\text{litres}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    These agree with the calculation in Example 12.4.5: reading a curve drawn with straight segments is a check on the arithmetic.

    6. Challenge: Working Backwards.

    A data set has quartile deviation \(4\) and lower quartile \(23\text{.}\) Find the upper quartile. Every value is then increased by \(10\text{.}\) What is the new interquartile range?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Adding the same number to every value moves both quartiles by that number.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(Q_3 = 31\text{;}\) the IQR stays \(8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    IQR \(= 2 \times 4 = 8\text{,}\) so \(Q_3 = 23 + 8 = 31\text{.}\) After adding \(10\text{,}\) \(Q_1 = 33\) and \(Q_3 = 41\text{:}\) the IQR is still \(8\text{.}\)

    Subsection 12.4.2 Standard Deviation of Grouped Data: the Assumed Mean

    Learner Experience 12.4.13 How Spread Are the Goats?

    Use the masses of the \(50\) goats at Garissa market from Example 12.2.6: frequencies \(3, 7, 12, 14, 9, 5\) for the classes 15–19 to 40–44 kg.
    🔗
    1. Write down the midpoint of each class.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Multiply each midpoint by its frequency and add. Estimate the mean mass.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Is this the true mean mass of the \(50\) goats? Explain.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Work out \(\Sigma fx^2\) and use \(\sigma^2 = \dfrac{\Sigma fx^2}{\Sigma f} - \bar{x}^2\) to estimate the standard deviation.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 12.4.14 Grouped Data.

    For grouped data, let \(x\) be the class midpoint. Then
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{\Sigma fx}{\Sigma f}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{\Sigma fx^2}{\Sigma f} - \bar{x}^2, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    These are estimates, because every value is placed at the midpoint of its class. Keep \(\bar{x}\) unrounded when you find \(\sigma^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaway 12.4.15 Grouped Data with an Assumed Mean.

    Choose \(A\text{,}\) a midpoint of a class near the middle, and work with \(d = x - A\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = A + \frac{\Sigma fd}{\Sigma f}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{\Sigma fd^2}{\Sigma f} - \left(\frac{\Sigma fd}{\Sigma f}\right)^2\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Subtracting the same number \(A\) from every value moves the mean down by \(A\) and leaves the spread unchanged, so the standard deviation of \(x\) equals that of \(d\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12.4.16 Goats: Standard Deviation.

    Estimate the mean and standard deviation of the goat masses in Learner Experience 12.4.13.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{1520}{50} = 30.4, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{48\,480}{50} - 30.4^2 = 969.6 - 924.16 = 45.44, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{45.44} \approx 6.74 \end{equation*}
    The mean mass is about \(30.4\,\text{kg}\) and the standard deviation about \(6.74\,\text{kg}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    With an assumed mean \(A = 32\text{:}\) \(d = -15, -10, -5, 0, 5, 10\text{,}\) \(\Sigma fd = -80\) and \(\Sigma fd^2 = 2400\text{,}\) so \(\bar{x} = 32 + \dfrac{-80}{50} = 30.4\) and \(\sigma^2 = \dfrac{2400}{50} - (-1.6)^2 = 48 - 2.56 = 45.44\text{,}\) the same.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12.4.18 Boda Boda Earnings.

    The daily earnings, \(e\) Ksh, of \(40\) boda boda riders in Eldoret are shown below. Use an assumed mean of Ksh \(900\) to estimate the mean and standard deviation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = 900 + \frac{1400}{40} = 935, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{2\,840\,000}{40} - 35^2 = 71\,000 - 1225 = 69\,775, \qquad \sigma \approx 264.15 \end{equation*}
    The mean daily earnings are about Ksh \(935\text{,}\) with standard deviation about Ksh \(264.15\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 12.4.21 Step Deviations (Extension).

    When the class widths are equal, the deviations can also be divided by the width \(c\text{:}\) \(u = \dfrac{x - A}{c}\text{.}\) For the boda boda earnings, \(u = \dfrac{x - 900}{200}\) gives \(u = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\text{,}\) \(\Sigma fu = 7\) and \(\Sigma fu^2 = 71\text{.}\) Then \(\sigma_u^2 = \dfrac{71}{40} - \left(\dfrac{7}{40}\right)^2 = 1.744375\text{,}\) and multiplying back by \(c\) gives \(\sigma = 200 \times \sqrt{1.744375} \approx 264.15\text{,}\) as before. This method is beyond the KICD Grade 12 design.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12.4.22 Changing Every Value.

      🔗
      🔗
    2. A test was marked out of \(40\text{;}\) the marks had mean \(26\) and standard deviation \(6\text{.}\) The marks are changed to percentages by multiplying by \(2.5\text{.}\) Find the new mean and standard deviation.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Adding \(1.5\) to every reading moves the mean up by \(1.5\) but does not change the spread: mean \(25.5\,^{\circ}\text{C}\text{,}\) standard deviation \(2.5\,^{\circ}\text{C}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Multiplying every mark by \(2.5\) multiplies the mean and every distance from the mean by \(2.5\text{:}\) mean \(65\text{,}\) standard deviation \(15\text{.}\)

    In general, if every value \(x\) becomes \(ax + b\) with \(a \gt 0\text{,}\) the mean becomes \(a\bar{x} + b\) and the standard deviation becomes \(a\sigma\text{.}\)

    7. Challenge: Two Formulas, One Variance.

    Show that \(\Sigma (x - \bar{x})^2 = \Sigma x^2 - n\bar{x}^2\text{,}\) so the two formulas for the variance always agree.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Expand \((x - \bar{x})^2\) and use \(\Sigma x = n\bar{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Expand and use \(\Sigma x = n\bar{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\Sigma (x - \bar{x})^2 = \Sigma x^2 - 2\bar{x}\Sigma x + n\bar{x}^2 = \Sigma x^2 - 2n\bar{x}^2 + n\bar{x}^2 = \Sigma x^2 - n\bar{x}^2\text{.}\) Dividing by \(n\) gives \(\dfrac{\Sigma (x - \bar{x})^2}{n} = \dfrac{\Sigma x^2}{n} - \bar{x}^2\text{.}\)

    Key Takeaway 12.4.29 Comparing Data Sets.

    To compare two data sets, compare a measure of centre (mean or median) and a measure of spread (standard deviation or interquartile range), and say what each means in the context. The data set with the smaller spread is the more consistent.
    🔗
    The mean and standard deviation use every value, so an extreme value pulls them. The median and interquartile range are not affected by extreme values, so they suit data with a few unusual values, such as incomes. Always say which measures you used and why.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12.4.30 Two Runners.

    Achieng and Mutua train at a camp in Iten. Their times, in seconds, for \(400\,\text{m}\) in eight races were
    🔗
    Compare the two runners.

    Example 12.4.32 Two Farms.

    Over many seasons, Farm P in Trans Nzoia had a median yield of \(24\) bags of maize per acre with an interquartile range of \(6\) bags. Farm Q had a median of \(26\) bags with an interquartile range of \(13\) bags. Compare the farms.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Farm Q has the higher typical yield (median \(26\) against \(24\) bags), but its yields vary much more (IQR \(13\) against \(6\)), so it has more poor seasons as well as more good ones. A farmer who cannot risk a bad year would prefer Farm P’s methods; one who can absorb a poor season might prefer Farm Q’s.
